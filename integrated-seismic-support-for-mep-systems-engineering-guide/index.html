Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems: Engineering Guide

Modern buildings depend on extensive mechanical, electrical, and plumbing infrastructure, yet these systems are often more vulnerable to earthquake-induced movement than the primary structure surrounding them. HVAC ductwork, piping, conduit, cable trays, suspended equipment, and mechanical units must remain supported and appropriately restrained as the building moves. That requirement makes seismic support an engineering problem involving much more than the installation of diagonal braces.

integrated seismic support for mep systems brings support geometry, seismic restraint, bracing, connections, anchorage, and the supporting structure into one coordinated load path. Instead of treating each hanger or brace as an isolated component, the design considers how seismic demand travels from an MEP component through its support assembly and ultimately into the building structure.

This approach is particularly important on hospitals, data centers, industrial facilities, high-rise buildings, laboratories, infrastructure facilities, and other projects where MEP continuity and structural coordination are critical. It also becomes increasingly valuable when multiple trades compete for limited ceiling space or when existing buildings require seismic retrofit work.

For engineers and contractors, the objective is not simply to specify a seismic-rated product. The objective is to develop a support system whose components, connections, structural attachments, geometry, and installation conditions work together as intended.

What Is Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems?

Integrated seismic support is a coordinated engineering strategy for supporting and restraining nonstructural mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems against applicable seismic demands. Conventional MEP supports primarily carry dead weight, maintain elevation, accommodate thermal movement, or position equipment and services. Seismic restraints introduce another function: controlling movement and transferring applicable earthquake-induced forces through an engineered load path.

Support vs. Restraint vs. Bracing

An MEP support carries the weight of a component and maintains its position. A seismic restraint limits movement under applicable seismic demand. A brace transfers restraint forces toward an attachment point or structural member. These functions can exist within one coordinated assembly, but they should not be treated as interchangeable.

For example, a threaded-rod trapeze may support a group of pipes under gravity loading while separate lateral and longitudinal restraints address seismic movement. Similarly, an equipment support frame may carry an air-handling unit while its anchorage and restraint system is designed for applicable seismic forces.

Vibration isolation is a separate engineering function. Spring isolators, wire rope isolators, rubber/metal isolators, and similar devices are intended primarily to reduce transmission of operational vibration and structure-borne noise. They may need seismic restraints or seismic-compatible interfaces, but an isolator should not automatically be assumed to provide seismic restraint.

The Complete Seismic Load Path

A useful way to understand integrated design is:

MEP Component → Support → Restraint → Brace → Connection → Anchor → Primary Structure

Every link matters. A strong brace attached to an inadequately designed support is not a complete seismic system. Likewise, a suitable anchor cannot compensate for an attachment connected to a structural member with insufficient capacity.

This load-path perspective is central to engineering seismic support systems for HVAC, piping, conduit, cable trays, and equipment.

Why MEP Systems Require Integrated Seismic Support

MEP systems respond to earthquake movement differently from the building's primary structural frame. Their mass, flexibility, support spacing, attachment conditions, operating configuration, and relationship to the building can affect their seismic response. A suspended duct, for example, does not behave like a rigidly anchored piece of floor-mounted equipment.

Seismic Response of Nonstructural Components

During an earthquake, the supporting structure moves and accelerates. MEP components attached to that structure experience corresponding demands, along with effects associated with their own mass and connection configuration. Flexible components may experience relative displacement, while rigidly attached equipment can transfer significant forces through its anchors.

The applicable seismic design approach depends on the project and component. ASCE 7 provisions for nonstructural components, together with the adopted building code, project specifications, and engineer-of-record requirements, establish the framework for determining when and how seismic restraint is required.

This is why a universal statement that every MEP support requires identical bracing would be technically misleading. Requirements can vary with Seismic Design Category, component characteristics, occupancy, building conditions, project criteria, applicable exceptions, and the adopted code.

Consequences of an Incomplete Load Path

A common design error is focusing on the visible brace while overlooking what happens at its ends. Brace angle affects force components. Attachment locations affect structural demand. Anchor capacity affects load transfer. Support members must resist the resulting reactions without unacceptable deformation or failure.

Coordination is equally important. A brace that conflicts with a duct, pipe, cable tray, ceiling, access panel, or architectural feature may be impossible to install as designed.

Integrated seismic support therefore connects seismic calculations with actual constructability. The objective is a complete system that can be fabricated, installed, inspected, and maintained—not simply a calculated force on a drawing.

Seismic Support Design for HVAC, Piping, and Mechanical Systems

Mechanical systems often represent a significant portion of the MEP infrastructure requiring coordinated seismic support. Their configurations range from suspended ductwork and piping to heavy equipment with dedicated structural support frames.

HVAC Ductwork and Equipment

HVAC ductwork may require support and restraint configurations appropriate to its size, routing, weight, flexibility, and project-specific seismic criteria. Bracing must be coordinated with hanger locations and structural attachment points without compromising duct installation or access.

Mechanical equipment introduces additional considerations. Air-handling units, fans, chillers, boilers, pumps, cooling towers, and similar equipment may require dedicated support frames or anchorage. The engineering must consider equipment weight, center of gravity, mounting configuration, operational requirements, and applicable seismic demand.

Piping Systems

Chilled-water, hydronic, domestic-water, process, fire-protection, medical-gas, refrigerant, and steam piping can have different support and restraint requirements. Pipe diameter, material, insulation, operating contents, temperature, flexibility, and support spacing can all influence the final arrangement.

A seismic pipe-support design should therefore be coordinated with the actual piping system rather than treated as a generic brace schedule. Longitudinal and transverse restraint, hanger configuration, connection capacity, thermal movement, and clearance must be considered together.

Mechanical Equipment Anchorage

Equipment anchorage differs from suspended-system bracing. Floor-mounted equipment may transfer seismic forces through base plates, anchor bolts, housekeeping pads, equipment rails, or structural frames. Suspended equipment may require a different restraint strategy.

The engineering objective is to establish how the equipment reacts under the applicable loading and how those forces reach the supporting structure. This is particularly important for critical mechanical equipment where displacement could affect connected piping, ductwork, electrical connections, or equipment operation.

Integrated Seismic Support for Electrical and Plumbing Systems

Electrical and plumbing infrastructure can require the same disciplined load-path thinking used for mechanical systems, although the support configurations and component characteristics differ.

Electrical Conduit and Raceway

Conduit and raceways are commonly supported using individual hangers, trapeze assemblies, strut-channel systems, or combinations of these arrangements. Seismic restraint must be coordinated with the actual support geometry and structural attachment.

Multiple conduit runs may be consolidated into a trapeze, creating an efficient support arrangement, but the combined weight and resulting seismic demand must be considered. Brace locations also need to account for neighboring systems, access requirements, ceiling conditions, and structural framing.

Cable Tray Systems

Cable trays introduce additional coordination considerations because they can span significant distances and carry concentrated or distributed cable loads. Support spacing, tray configuration, cable loading, connections, and seismic restraint must be considered as part of the complete system.

Where multiple electrical pathways occupy the same area, integrated support assemblies can reduce congestion when properly engineered. However, shared support should never be assumed appropriate simply because components can physically fit together. The combined loading, attachment capacity, installation sequence, and applicable project requirements must be evaluated.

Plumbing and Specialty Systems

Plumbing systems may include domestic water, sanitary systems, specialty piping, fire protection, medical gas, and other services. Their seismic treatment depends on the applicable code provisions, project requirements, system characteristics, and building conditions.

For healthcare facilities, specialty MEP systems can have particularly important operational consequences. Seismic support design must therefore be coordinated with the facility's engineering requirements, structural conditions, equipment interfaces, and applicable HCAI requirements when the project falls within HCAI jurisdiction.

Seismic Bracing, Trapeze Systems, and Custom MEP Supports

Integrated support systems frequently combine standard components with engineered assemblies and custom-fabricated elements. The correct balance depends on loads, geometry, structural interfaces, installation conditions, and project specifications.

Seismic Trapeze Assemblies

A seismic trapeze can support multiple pipes, conduits, or other MEP services within a coordinated assembly. When properly designed, it can consolidate support infrastructure and simplify coordination across trades.

The engineering must account for the combined gravity and seismic demands. Hanger rods, strut members, fittings, clamps, braces, and structural attachments all contribute to the load path. The trapeze should be evaluated as an assembly rather than assuming each individual component will independently satisfy the design.

Strut Channel Systems

Strut channel provides a flexible platform for building MEP support assemblies. Channels, fittings, threaded rods, clamps, brackets, and brace components can be configured for a wide range of applications.

However, a generic strut arrangement is not automatically an engineered seismic support. The required configuration depends on loading, span, connection type, brace geometry, material properties, and structural attachment.

Custom Fabricated Support Frames

Custom fabrication becomes valuable when standard components cannot efficiently accommodate project-specific conditions. Examples include unusual equipment dimensions, congested ceiling spaces, retrofit constraints, offset structural connections, specialized mounting plates, or high-load support frames.

Custom support assemblies can be fabricated from carbon steel, structural steel, stainless steel, aluminum, or other appropriate materials. The selection should reflect structural requirements, corrosion exposure, temperature, fire considerations, project specifications, and installation environment.

Seismic Calculations, Connections, and Structural Anchorage

Seismic support design becomes meaningful only when calculated demands are translated into physical components and structural connections capable of carrying them.

Determining Seismic Demand

Applicable seismic design provisions establish the basis for evaluating nonstructural components. Depending on the project, this can involve building seismic parameters, component characteristics, component importance, response factors, amplification effects, and other criteria defined by the applicable code and project requirements.

ASCE 7 Chapter 13 is particularly relevant to seismic design of many nonstructural components. The adopted edition and applicable jurisdiction must always be verified rather than assuming that a particular edition applies universally.

Brace and Connection Loads

Brace geometry affects the forces delivered to the support and its attachments. A change in brace angle can alter the resulting force components. Support spacing can influence tributary mass. Multiple MEP services sharing an assembly can increase reactions.

These effects should be incorporated into seismic calculations rather than addressed after the support configuration has already been selected.

Anchorage to Concrete and Steel

Structural attachment is one of the most important parts of the load path. Depending on the existing structure and project conditions, attachments may involve cast-in-place anchors, post-installed mechanical or adhesive anchors, structural steel connections, beam clamps, welded plates, bolted connections, or custom attachment hardware.

The attachment must be compatible with the supporting structure and the calculated demand. An anchor should not be selected solely from its nominal product capacity without considering the actual concrete, steel, edge distance, embedment, installation conditions, load direction, and applicable evaluation requirements.

This is where seismic calculations and structural engineering must connect directly with fabrication and field installation.

BIM and 3D CAD Coordination for Integrated MEP Seismic Support

BIM and 3D CAD are particularly valuable when seismic supports occupy the same constrained spaces as other building systems.

MEP and Structural Coordination

A coordinated model can show whether a brace intersects ductwork, piping, conduit, cable trays, ceilings, lighting, structural framing, or architectural elements before fabrication begins.

This reduces the risk of designing a theoretically acceptable support that cannot actually be installed. It also allows structural attachment points to be coordinated earlier, reducing field modifications.

Fabrication and Installation Drawings

A properly developed 3D model can support fabrication-ready details, dimensions, connection information, support identifiers, and installation drawings. Custom brackets and strut assemblies can be modeled before material is cut.

For a fabrication-oriented engineering workflow, this connection between BIM and manufacturing is significant. Laser cutting, plasma cutting, forming, machining, and welding can be coordinated with the engineered geometry rather than relying on field improvisation.

Retrofit and Existing-Condition Coordination

Existing buildings create additional uncertainty. Structural framing may differ from legacy drawings, ceiling spaces may be congested, and existing MEP services can restrict attachment locations.

Field verification combined with 3D coordination can identify these constraints before fabrication. This is especially useful for hospitals, occupied commercial buildings, industrial facilities, and renovation projects where installation access is limited.

Integrating Seismic Restraint With Vibration Isolation

Vibration control and seismic protection often intersect at mechanical equipment, but they solve different engineering problems.

Vibration Isolation vs. Seismic Restraint

Spring isolators reduce the transmission of operational vibration by allowing controlled movement. Wire rope isolators and rubber/metal isolators can provide isolation characteristics appropriate to different equipment and environmental conditions. Captive configurations may be used where additional retention is required.

Seismic restraints address movement associated with seismic demand. Their geometry and stiffness must be compatible with the equipment's intended operation and the isolation system.

The two functions should therefore be coordinated rather than substituted for one another.

Coordinating Movement and Restraint

An isolated air-handling unit, pump, or other mechanical assembly needs sufficient operating movement for its isolation system to function. Adding an improperly configured rigid restraint can short-circuit the isolation system or introduce unwanted loads.

Conversely, failing to provide an appropriate seismic restraint strategy can leave isolated equipment inadequately protected against earthquake movement.

The engineering solution depends on equipment characteristics, isolation properties, seismic criteria, clearances, restraint configuration, and connected services. This makes the interface between vibration isolation and seismic restraint an important part of integrated MEP support design.

Integrated Seismic Support for Healthcare and High-Performance Facilities

Healthcare and mission-critical facilities often place greater emphasis on MEP continuity, coordination, documentation, and seismic performance.

HCAI and California Healthcare Projects

California healthcare projects that fall within the jurisdiction of the Department of Health Care Access and Information may have requirements beyond those encountered on ordinary commercial projects. HCAI is the current agency name; OSHPD remains common in legacy drawings, documentation, and historical pre-approval terminology.

Seismic support systems on these projects must be evaluated against the applicable HCAI requirements, project documents, structural design, and adopted code provisions. HCAI applicability should never be assumed simply because a project is located in California.

Mission-Critical MEP Systems

Hospitals, data centers, laboratories, emergency facilities, and industrial process buildings may depend heavily on continued operation of mechanical and electrical services. Failure of a support system can cause secondary damage even when the primary structural frame remains functional.

This increases the importance of coordinated equipment support, piping restraint, ductwork bracing, electrical support, structural attachment, and inspection documentation.

Documentation and Coordination

Engineering calculations, coordinated drawings, product information, structural attachment details, fabrication documents, and installation requirements should support the final system. The exact documentation depends on the project and governing requirements.

Materials, Hardware, and Fabrication of Seismic MEP Supports

Material selection for seismic support assemblies should follow engineering requirements rather than a one-size-fits-all specification.

Structural Steel and Strut Channel

Carbon steel and structural steel are common choices for support frames and heavier assemblies. Galvanized steel can be appropriate where corrosion protection is required, while stainless steel may be selected for demanding environments or compatibility requirements.

Aluminum and specialized materials can also be appropriate in selected applications, but their use must account for strength, stiffness, connection behavior, corrosion compatibility, and project requirements.

Connections and Hardware

Threaded rods, clamps, strut fittings, structural bolts, anchor bolts, beam attachments, welded plates, and custom brackets can all become part of an integrated support assembly.

Their suitability depends on actual loading and connection design. Hardware should be evaluated as part of the complete load path rather than selected independently based only on nominal size.

Custom Metal Fabrication

Custom fabrication allows engineered support designs to be translated into repeatable physical assemblies. Laser and plasma cutting can produce accurately dimensioned plates and components; forming can create brackets and channels; machining can produce precision connection features; and welding can create structural frames and assemblies.

Galvanizing and powder coating may provide appropriate finishes for selected environments, subject to project specifications and material compatibility.

The value of fabrication in an integrated seismic workflow is not simply manufacturing capacity. It is the ability to maintain consistency between engineering geometry, fabrication dimensions, connection details, and field installation.

Integrated Seismic Support for New Construction and Retrofit Projects

The engineering process differs significantly between a new building and an existing facility.

New Construction

New construction provides greater opportunity to coordinate seismic support locations with primary structural framing, architectural layouts, MEP routing, and ceiling systems. Structural attachment points can be considered earlier, and BIM coordination can identify conflicts before installation.

Early coordination can also reduce unnecessary offsets, improve brace placement, and minimize field modifications.

Existing Buildings

Retrofit projects may involve limited structural attachment locations, unknown existing conditions, occupied spaces, inaccessible framing, and legacy MEP installations. A theoretically efficient support design may not be feasible once field conditions are verified.

Field measurements and existing-condition assessment are therefore important. Custom support frames, brackets, or alternative attachment strategies may be needed to accommodate the existing structure.

Constructability and Installation Sequencing

A seismic support system must be installable. Brace access, fastening sequence, clearance, trade coordination, lifting requirements, and inspection access can all affect the final configuration.

Engineering should therefore consider how the system will actually be assembled in the field. This connection between design and constructability is particularly important when custom fabricated support assemblies are used.

How to Plan an Integrated MEP Seismic Support Project

A successful project begins with engineering criteria rather than hardware selection. The following workflow provides a practical framework.

  1. Identify the building and applicable criteria. Establish the building type, jurisdiction, adopted code, Seismic Design Category, project specifications, and applicable owner or engineer requirements.

  2. Classify the MEP components. Identify HVAC equipment, ductwork, piping, conduit, cable trays, plumbing systems, and other nonstructural components requiring evaluation.

  3. Review structural and MEP documentation. Examine structural framing, architectural constraints, MEP routing, equipment information, and available attachment locations.

  4. Determine applicable seismic demand. Establish the appropriate design parameters and component-specific requirements using the governing criteria.

  5. Develop support and restraint configurations. Determine support spacing, hanger arrangements, restraint points, brace directions, and equipment anchorage.

  6. Evaluate brace and connection forces. Account for geometry, tributary mass, brace angles, fittings, and support reactions.

  7. Verify structural attachments. Check concrete, steel, anchors, welded connections, bolted connections, or other structural interfaces.

  8. Coordinate through BIM or 3D CAD. Identify conflicts and confirm clearances before fabrication.

  9. Develop fabrication and installation drawings. Translate engineered geometry into details that can be manufactured and installed consistently.

  10. Fabricate custom assemblies where required. Use appropriate cutting, forming, machining, welding, and finishing processes.

  11. Perform field verification. Confirm existing conditions, attachment locations, dimensions, and installation constraints.

  12. Coordinate installation and inspection. Ensure the installed system reflects the engineered configuration and project documentation.

  13. Document the completed system. Maintain the calculations, drawings, product information, fabrication details, and inspection records required by the project.

This workflow turns integrated seismic support into a controlled engineering process rather than a collection of individually selected braces and supports.

Common Integrated MEP Seismic Support Mistakes

Several recurring problems can undermine otherwise sophisticated seismic support designs.

The first is treating ordinary MEP supports as seismic restraints. A gravity support may carry the weight of a pipe or duct while providing little resistance to the movement associated with an earthquake.

The second is adding braces without verifying the complete load path. Brace capacity alone does not establish system adequacy. The support member, fitting, attachment, anchor, and structural member must all be considered.

Another issue is ignoring structural attachment capacity. Attaching a seismic brace to a convenient location does not automatically make that location structurally adequate.

Improper anchor selection is another frequent concern. Concrete strength, embedment, edge distance, cracking conditions, installation requirements, and load direction can affect anchor performance.

Designers may also overlook brace geometry and spacing. A change in angle, support configuration, or tributary mass can significantly alter reactions.

Other common issues include failing to distinguish between mechanical, electrical, and plumbing applications; neglecting equipment-specific requirements; overlooking vibration-isolation movement; using generic strut configurations without engineering verification; and failing to coordinate braces with other MEP trades.

Retrofit projects introduce additional risks when drawings do not accurately reflect field conditions. A support designed around assumed structural framing may require modification once the actual building is opened.

Finally, code references themselves must be handled carefully. IBC, CBC, and ASCE 7 provisions should be applied according to their scope, the adopted edition, project conditions, and applicable requirements. HCAI requirements should likewise be applied to projects within the agency's jurisdiction rather than treated as universal requirements for every California building.

Conclusion: Designing the Complete MEP Seismic Load Path

Integrated seismic support for MEP systems is fundamentally an exercise in coordinated load-path engineering. The visible component may be a brace, trapeze, hanger, bracket, or strut assembly, but its performance depends on the entire chain connecting the MEP system to the primary structure.

That chain can be expressed as:

MEP Component → Support → Restraint → Brace → Connection → Anchor → Structure

A reliable design must account for the component's seismic demand, support geometry, restraint configuration, brace forces, connection reactions, anchorage, structural capacity, and installation conditions. It must also recognize the differences between ordinary support, seismic restraint, seismic bracing, equipment anchorage, and vibration isolation.

For HVAC systems, this means coordinating ductwork, equipment, piping, and support frames. For electrical systems, it means addressing conduit, raceways, cable trays, and associated support assemblies. For plumbing and specialty systems, the design must reflect the specific component, building conditions, and governing requirements.

BIM and 3D CAD add another important dimension by connecting engineering analysis with constructability and fabrication. Custom strut channels, brackets, trapezes, and structural support frames can then be developed around actual project geometry rather than forcing the installation to conform to generic hardware.

For California healthcare and other regulated facilities, the process may also require careful coordination with HCAI requirements and project-specific documentation. Across the broader U.S. market, the applicable adopted building code, ASCE 7 provisions, project specifications, and engineer-of-record requirements should establish the governing design framework.

FAQ: Integrated Seismic Support for MEP Systems

What is integrated seismic support for MEP systems?

Integrated seismic support is an engineered approach that considers MEP support, seismic restraint, bracing, connections, anchorage, and the supporting structure as one coordinated system. Instead of designing a brace independently from the hanger or structural attachment, the engineer evaluates how seismic forces move from the MEP component through the complete load path into the building structure.

This approach can apply to HVAC ductwork, piping, mechanical equipment, electrical conduit, cable trays, plumbing systems, and other applicable nonstructural components. The specific requirements depend on the building, component, seismic criteria, adopted code, project specifications, and applicable exceptions.

How is integrated seismic support different from standard MEP support?

Standard MEP supports commonly address gravity loads, positioning, thermal movement, installation requirements, and equipment support. Seismic support adds requirements associated with applicable earthquake-induced forces and movement.

A pipe hanger, for example, may adequately support the pipe's weight but not provide the lateral or longitudinal restraint needed under applicable seismic design criteria. An integrated system evaluates both functions and establishes how the resulting forces transfer through the support assembly and into the structure.

What MEP systems may require seismic bracing or restraint?

Potential applications include HVAC ductwork, chilled-water and hydronic piping, plumbing systems, electrical conduit, cable trays, mechanical equipment, electrical equipment, and specialty systems. However, it would be incorrect to assume that every MEP component receives identical seismic treatment.

Requirements can depend on the building's Seismic Design Category, component characteristics, occupancy, project criteria, applicable code provisions, equipment configuration, and other factors. Engineering evaluation should determine what restraint or anchorage is applicable to each system.

How are seismic loads calculated for MEP components?

The calculation methodology depends on the governing code, adopted edition, project conditions, and type of component. For applicable nonstructural components, ASCE 7 provides seismic design provisions that can establish component forces using building and component-specific parameters.

The resulting demand must then be translated into actual support reactions, brace forces, connection loads, and anchorage requirements. The calculation should therefore connect directly to the physical support configuration rather than existing as an isolated theoretical value.

What is the seismic load path for an MEP support system?

A simplified load path is:

MEP Component → Support → Restraint → Brace → Connection → Anchor → Primary Structure

Every element must be compatible with the forces being transferred. If a brace is adequate but its connection is weak, the load path is incomplete. Similarly, an appropriate anchor does not solve a problem caused by inadequate supporting concrete or steel.

This is why integrated seismic support considers the entire assembly instead of evaluating only the most visible brace.

Do HVAC ducts and piping require the same seismic support configuration?

No. HVAC ductwork and piping have different physical characteristics, support arrangements, operating conditions, and connection requirements. Piping can also experience thermal expansion and movement that must be coordinated with restraint locations.

Ductwork, meanwhile, may have different support geometries, dimensions, pressure characteristics, and attachment conditions. Both systems should be designed according to their actual characteristics and applicable seismic requirements rather than applying a generic configuration to every MEP service.

Can cable trays and conduit use integrated seismic trapeze supports?

They can be incorporated into engineered trapeze or support assemblies when the configuration is appropriate for the project. A shared trapeze can support multiple services, potentially improving coordination in congested MEP spaces.

However, combining services also combines their loads and reactions. The support member, fittings, threaded rods, braces, structural attachment, and anchor must be evaluated for the resulting demands. Physical compatibility alone does not establish engineering adequacy.

How are MEP seismic supports connected to concrete or structural steel?

Possible attachment methods include cast-in-place anchors, post-installed mechanical or adhesive anchors, beam attachments, structural clamps, welded connections, bolted connections, and custom steel plates or brackets.

The appropriate solution depends on the existing structure and calculated demand. Concrete strength, anchor embedment, edge distance, cracking conditions, steel member capacity, connection geometry, installation conditions, and applicable evaluation requirements can all affect the design.

Can vibration isolation systems be combined with seismic restraints?

Yes, but the two systems must be coordinated carefully. Vibration isolators are intended to allow controlled movement and reduce operational vibration transmission, while seismic restraints address applicable earthquake-induced movement.

A restraint that is too rigid or improperly positioned can interfere with the intended isolation behavior. Conversely, an isolated equipment assembly may require a dedicated seismic restraint strategy. Spring isolators, wire rope isolators, rubber/metal isolators, and captive configurations should therefore be evaluated alongside the equipment's seismic requirements rather than independently.

What role does BIM play in MEP seismic support design?

BIM and 3D CAD allow seismic support geometry to be coordinated with structural framing, ducts, pipes, conduit, cable trays, ceilings, equipment, access zones, and neighboring trades.

This can identify conflicts before fabrication and installation. For custom supports, the model can also provide the dimensional information needed for fabrication drawings and engineered assemblies. In retrofit work, BIM can be combined with field verification to represent existing conditions more accurately.

Are integrated seismic supports required for healthcare and HCAI projects?

Healthcare projects within HCAI jurisdiction may have specific requirements affecting nonstructural components, seismic restraint, structural attachments, documentation, and inspection. HCAI is the current California agency name, while OSHPD remains common in older project documentation and legacy pre-approval terminology.

The exact requirements should be established for the individual project. HCAI requirements should not be assumed to apply universally to every healthcare-related building or every MEP support.

Can custom strut channels and fabricated supports be used for MEP seismic systems?

Yes. Custom strut channels, trapezes, brackets, equipment frames, mounting plates, and other fabricated assemblies can be appropriate when standard support products do not provide the required geometry, capacity, or structural interface.

The custom assembly should be engineered as a complete system, including material properties, member dimensions, connections, brace geometry, structural attachment, and fabrication requirements. This approach is particularly useful for high-load applications, unusual structural interfaces, congested MEP spaces, and retrofit projects where standard configurations are difficult to install.
