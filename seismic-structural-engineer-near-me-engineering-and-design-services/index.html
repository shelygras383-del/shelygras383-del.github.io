What Does a Seismic Structural Engineer Do?

A seismic structural engineer evaluates how a structure will resist and transfer earthquake-induced forces through its structural system. The work begins with defining the applicable seismic design criteria and continues through analysis, member design, connection design, anchorage, detailing, documentation, and construction coordination.

Unlike a generic structural calculation focused on gravity loads, seismic structural engineering must consider dynamic structural response and the way lateral forces move through the building. Depending on the structure, this can involve moment-resisting frames, braced frames, shear walls, diaphragms, collectors, columns, beams, foundations, and structural connections.

Structural Engineering vs. Seismic Structural Engineering

Structural engineering encompasses many types of loading and structural behavior, including gravity, wind, seismic, thermal, and other project-specific demands. Seismic engineering focuses specifically on earthquake-related response and the associated strength, stiffness, ductility, drift, load path, and detailing requirements.

A seismic structural engineer may perform seismic load calculations, analyze structural response, evaluate existing framing, design new structural members, investigate vulnerabilities, or develop retrofit solutions. The engineer may also assess how equipment, architectural elements, and MEP systems interact with the primary structure.

From Seismic Demand to Structural Capacity

A useful way to understand the engineering workflow is:

Seismic Hazard → Building Response → Seismic Demand → Structural Members → Connections → Anchorage → Foundation

Each link matters. Designing an adequately sized beam does not by itself establish an adequate seismic system if the connection, diaphragm, collector, column, anchor, or foundation cannot transfer the resulting forces. This complete-load-path approach is fundamental to reliable seismic structural design.

When Do You Need a Seismic Structural Engineer?

A seismic structural engineer may be required for both new construction and existing-building projects, particularly when earthquake resistance is a significant component of the structural design or when modifications affect the existing load path.

For new construction, seismic engineering begins during structural system selection. The architectural configuration, building height, occupancy, materials, site conditions, and lateral-force-resisting system can all influence the design approach. Early structural coordination can prevent later conflicts involving columns, shear walls, braces, mechanical equipment, openings, and architectural layouts.

New Construction

Commercial buildings, industrial facilities, healthcare buildings, warehouses, institutional facilities, and infrastructure projects may require project-specific seismic analysis. The engineer establishes applicable design criteria and develops a structural system capable of resisting the required demands.

The work may include steel framing, reinforced-concrete framing, shear walls, moment frames, braced frames, diaphragms, foundations, and connections. Seismic calculations and structural drawings must be coordinated with architectural, MEP, and construction requirements.

Existing Buildings and Renovations

Existing structures create a different engineering challenge. Available drawings may be incomplete, construction may differ from original documents, and materials or connections may not be fully documented. A renovation can also alter structural behavior by removing walls, creating openings, adding equipment, changing occupancy, or modifying structural members.

Seismic Assessment and Retrofit

A seismic assessment may identify vulnerabilities that require further engineering investigation. Depending on the building and applicable evaluation criteria, retrofit engineering can involve strengthening existing members, improving connections, modifying diaphragms, adding lateral-resisting elements, or upgrading structural anchorage. The appropriate solution depends on the building, project objectives, existing conditions, and governing requirements rather than a universal retrofit formula.

How Seismic Structural Design Works

Seismic structural design starts with project-specific criteria rather than selecting a generic earthquake load. Location, site characteristics, building use, structural configuration, and applicable code requirements influence the seismic design parameters.

Seismic Design Criteria

The design process can involve parameters such as seismic hazard values, Site Class, Risk Category, Seismic Design Category, response characteristics, and the selected analytical procedure. The applicable adopted code and jurisdiction must be verified because code editions and local amendments can affect project requirements.

Seismic Load Calculation

Depending on the structure, seismic analysis may use an equivalent lateral force procedure, modal response spectrum analysis, or another applicable analytical approach. Calculations can establish design forces and responses that must subsequently be distributed through diaphragms and the lateral-force-resisting system.

Base shear is only one part of the engineering picture. Engineers also examine story forces, member demands, overturning, torsion, drift, stiffness, and load combinations. Structural response can vary significantly depending on the building's geometry and lateral system.

Structural Response

A building must have sufficient strength and appropriate stiffness to respond to seismic demand without unacceptable structural behavior. Ductility and detailing can be particularly important in systems intended to undergo significant inelastic response.

Irregular configurations may require additional attention because discontinuities in stiffness, strength, geometry, or mass can influence seismic response. This is why seismic design cannot be reduced to applying a single force to a building. It is an interconnected analysis of how the entire structural system behaves.

Understanding the Seismic Load Path

The seismic load path is one of the most important concepts when evaluating structural engineering quality. Earthquake-induced forces must travel through connected structural elements to the foundation and supporting ground without relying on an incomplete or poorly defined transfer mechanism.

Lateral-Force-Resisting Systems

A building may rely on a moment-resisting frame, braced frame, shear wall system, or combination of systems. Structural diaphragms distribute lateral forces to these vertical resisting elements, while collectors and chords can transfer forces within the diaphragm system.

In a steel building, for example, beams, columns, braces, connections, plates, and anchors must work together. In reinforced concrete construction, slabs, beams, columns, shear walls, reinforcement, foundations, and their interfaces must provide a continuous structural mechanism.

Members and Connections

Member capacity is only meaningful when connections can transfer the required forces. Bolted and welded connections, base plates, embed plates, brace connections, and structural bolts therefore form an important part of seismic design.

Foundation and Anchorage

The load path ultimately reaches the foundation. Foundation design and anchorage may involve concrete capacity, reinforcement, anchor bolts, post-installed anchors, soil conditions, overturning effects, and other project-specific considerations.

For equipment and MEP applications, a related secondary load path may be:

MEP Component → Support → Seismic Restraint → Structural Attachment → Primary Structure

This distinction is important because a conventional support may carry gravity loads without automatically providing the seismic restraint required for earthquake movement.

Seismic Structural Engineering for Existing Buildings

Existing-building work requires an understanding of both current structural behavior and the limitations of available information. A seismic structural engineer may need to review original drawings, previous modifications, field conditions, material information, structural details, and the intended scope of the new project before determining an appropriate evaluation strategy.

Existing-Condition Assessment

Field verification can be essential when record drawings do not fully represent actual construction. Engineers may need to verify framing dimensions, member sizes, connection conditions, structural modifications, deterioration, equipment loads, and other conditions that influence analysis.

The assessment should distinguish documented conditions from assumptions. If a critical structural element cannot be verified, the uncertainty may affect subsequent calculations or require additional investigation.

Identifying Seismic Vulnerabilities

Potential vulnerabilities can include discontinuous load paths, inadequate connections, deterioration, weak or soft stories, diaphragm deficiencies, irregular structural configurations, insufficient lateral resistance, or changes made after original construction.

Not every observed deficiency requires the same response. The significance depends on the structural system, project objectives, applicable evaluation criteria, demand, capacity, and consequences of the condition.

Retrofit Strategies

Seismic retrofit engineering may involve structural strengthening intended to improve the building's response. Potential measures can include strengthening existing framing, adding or modifying shear walls, improving connections, reinforcing diaphragms, improving collectors, or upgrading anchorage.

ASCE 41 may be relevant to existing-building seismic evaluation and retrofit strategies where its provisions are applicable. Retrofit design must also account for constructability, existing foundations, architectural constraints, occupied spaces, utilities, and construction sequencing.

Seismic Retrofit Engineering and Structural Strengthening

Seismic retrofit is not simply the process of adding more steel or concrete to an existing building. A successful retrofit strategy begins with understanding the building's existing structural system, identifying the governing deficiencies, and determining how modifications will change the overall load path and structural response.

When Retrofit May Be Considered

Retrofit engineering can become relevant during building renovation, changes in occupancy or use, major alterations, structural modifications, owner-driven risk reduction, or evaluation of an existing structure. The exact triggers depend on the jurisdiction, adopted code provisions, project scope, and applicable requirements.

The engineer must determine whether a proposed modification affects the existing lateral system, gravity system, diaphragm, connections, foundations, or other structural elements.

Retrofit Design Constraints

Occupied facilities introduce additional constraints. Hospitals, manufacturing plants, commercial buildings, and other active facilities may not allow unrestricted access to structural elements. Retrofit work may need to be sequenced around operations, utilities, architectural finishes, equipment, and safety requirements.

For this reason, structural strengthening should be coordinated with architecture and MEP disciplines early in design. A structurally effective retrofit that cannot be constructed without major conflicts may require redesign.

ASCE 41 and Existing Buildings

Where applicable, ASCE 41 provides a framework for seismic evaluation and retrofit of existing buildings. It should be used within its intended scope and in coordination with the governing project requirements. The applicable standard, code provisions, evaluation objectives, and authority having jurisdiction should be confirmed for each project.

Structural Connections, Anchorage, and Nonstructural Components

Seismic structural engineering extends beyond beams, columns, and shear walls. Connections and anchorage are critical because they determine how seismic forces transfer between individual elements and into the supporting structure.

Steel and Concrete Connections

Steel structures can incorporate bolted and welded connections, brace connections, base plates, structural plates, angles, channels, and other components. Reinforced-concrete systems may rely on reinforcement, embedded plates, anchors, dowels, and concrete-to-steel interfaces.

Connection design must account for the forces actually generated by the structural system and the applicable material and seismic design requirements. A connection cannot be evaluated independently of the member and load path around it.

Equipment Anchorage

Equipment anchorage is another specialized application. Mechanical, electrical, and industrial equipment may impose seismic forces on supporting slabs, frames, housekeeping pads, or structural members. Anchor selection and design must consider the equipment configuration, support geometry, substrate, loads, and applicable requirements.

MEP Seismic Support

Suspended HVAC ductwork, piping, conduit, and cable trays may require seismic restraint or bracing as nonstructural components. A typical pathway is:

Component → Hanger or Support → Seismic Brace → Attachment → Structure

MEP seismic support should be coordinated with the primary structure so that attachment points have sufficient capacity and the complete load path is understood.

Seismic Codes and Engineering Standards

Seismic structural engineering depends on applying the correct standards to the correct part of the project. No single standard governs every engineering decision, and the applicable adopted editions and local amendments should be verified before design begins.

ASCE 7

ASCE 7 contains seismic loading provisions used to establish earthquake-related design demands for structures and applicable components. It addresses parameters and procedures that support determination of seismic forces and structural response.

The engineer must select the provisions appropriate to the building and analysis method rather than treating ASCE 7 as a standalone design manual for every structural material and connection.

IBC and CBC

The International Building Code establishes model building requirements that jurisdictions may adopt and amend. California projects are subject to the applicable California Building Code and related California Building Standards Code provisions.

The governing code edition, local amendments, project jurisdiction, and authority having jurisdiction should be confirmed for each project. Historical code requirements should not automatically be substituted for the requirements applicable to current work.

AISC and ACI

Steel structural design commonly requires coordination with AISC standards, including applicable provisions for structural steel and seismic systems. Reinforced-concrete design is commonly coordinated with ACI requirements.

These standards address material-specific design, detailing, strength, connections, and other structural considerations that complement seismic loading requirements.

HCAI and OSHPD

California healthcare projects within HCAI jurisdiction have additional requirements that must be addressed according to the scope and type of work. OSHPD is the historical agency name that continues to appear in legacy documentation and terminology, while HCAI is the current agency name.

FAQ: Seismic Structural Engineer Near Me

What does a seismic structural engineer do?

A seismic structural engineer analyzes how a building or structure responds to earthquake-induced forces and develops structural solutions appropriate to the project's seismic demands. The work can include establishing seismic design criteria, calculating seismic forces, analyzing the lateral-force-resisting system, checking structural members, designing connections and anchorage, evaluating foundations, and preparing engineering documentation.

For existing buildings, the scope may include field verification, seismic assessment, vulnerability identification, and retrofit engineering. For new construction, the engineer may participate in structural system selection, seismic analysis, member design, connection design, and coordination with architecture and MEP disciplines. The specific scope depends on the project and applicable requirements.

How do I find a seismic structural engineer near me?

Searching for a seismic structural engineer near me can identify geographically relevant engineering firms, but proximity should not be the only selection criterion. Review whether the engineer has experience with the building type, structural system, seismic conditions, jurisdiction, and engineering deliverables required for the project.

It is also useful to determine whether the engineering team can coordinate with architects, MEP contractors, general contractors, fabricators, and facility personnel. For complex projects, capabilities such as seismic calculations, existing-building assessment, BIM coordination, connection design, anchorage, and construction support may be more important than physical distance alone.

When does a building need seismic structural engineering?

Seismic structural engineering may be required when designing a new structure in a region with applicable seismic design requirements, modifying an existing lateral-force-resisting system, evaluating an existing building, planning a seismic retrofit, or introducing structural changes that affect seismic behavior.

Major renovations, additions, changes in occupancy, new equipment, structural openings, removal of walls or framing, and other modifications can also create a need for engineering review. Whether a specific project requires a particular analysis or design procedure depends on the adopted code, jurisdiction, project scope, and authority having jurisdiction.

What is included in a seismic structural analysis?

A seismic structural analysis can include development of seismic design criteria, determination of seismic forces, selection of an applicable analysis procedure, modeling of the structural system, evaluation of member demands, and assessment of drift and overall structural response.

Depending on the project, engineers may also examine torsion, diaphragm behavior, collectors, overturning, load combinations, connection forces, anchorage, and foundation demands. The analysis must be appropriate to the structural configuration and governing requirements. A seismic calculation is therefore more than a single force value; it is part of an interconnected evaluation of structural behavior and load transfer.

How are seismic loads calculated for a building?

Seismic loads are determined using project-specific information and the applicable design standard. Factors can include geographic seismic hazard, site conditions, Site Class, Risk Category, Seismic Design Category, structural system, building characteristics, and the applicable analytical procedure.

Depending on the building, an engineer may use an equivalent lateral force procedure, modal response spectrum analysis, or another appropriate method. The resulting forces and responses must then be distributed through the structural system and transferred through members, connections, anchorage, and foundations. The applicable adopted code and project criteria determine which procedures and parameters are appropriate.

What is the difference between seismic design and seismic retrofit engineering?

Seismic design generally refers to developing a structural system for a new building or new structural work so that it satisfies applicable seismic requirements. The engineer can select the framing system, member sizes, connections, diaphragms, foundations, and other components as part of an integrated design.

Seismic retrofit engineering deals with an existing structure. The engineer must first understand the existing building, identify relevant deficiencies or project-specific vulnerabilities, and then develop modifications that improve structural performance while accounting for existing conditions. Retrofit work can be constrained by occupied spaces, foundations, architectural layouts, utilities, construction sequencing, and the condition of existing structural components.

What codes and standards do seismic structural engineers use?

The standards used depend on the project and jurisdiction. ASCE 7 contains important seismic loading provisions, while the applicable building code establishes requirements adopted by the governing jurisdiction. In California, the California Building Code and related California Building Standards Code provisions may apply.

Material-specific design can require standards such as AISC for structural steel and ACI for reinforced concrete. ASCE 41 can be relevant to evaluation and retrofit of existing buildings where applicable. Healthcare projects within HCAI jurisdiction may have additional requirements. The engineer should verify the current adopted editions, amendments, project specifications, and authority having jurisdiction rather than assuming that one standard applies universally.

Does ASCE 7 apply to existing buildings?

ASCE 7 is primarily a standard for minimum design loads and associated criteria for structures and other elements within its scope; it should not automatically be treated as a standalone existing-building evaluation standard. Existing-building assessment and retrofit may involve other applicable standards, including ASCE 41 where appropriate, together with the governing building code and project requirements.

The correct approach depends on why the existing building is being evaluated, the extent of alterations, the jurisdiction, and the objectives established for the project. An engineer should determine which standards and provisions apply rather than applying a new-construction methodology without considering the existing-building context.

What is a seismic load path in structural engineering?

A seismic load path is the continuous route through which earthquake-induced forces are transferred through a structure to its foundation and supporting ground. In a typical conceptual pathway, seismic demand is transferred through floor or roof diaphragms to collectors and vertical lateral-force-resisting elements, then through structural members and connections into the foundation.

Connections and anchorage are therefore just as important as major structural members. If one part of the path lacks adequate strength, stiffness, detailing, or attachment capacity, the overall seismic system may not perform as intended. For MEP and equipment applications, a related path can extend from the component through its support and seismic restraint to the structural attachment and primary structure.

Can a seismic structural engineer evaluate an existing building?

Yes. Existing-building evaluation can be an important part of seismic structural engineering. The process may involve reviewing available drawings, conducting field verification, documenting structural conditions, identifying modifications, determining material information, and analyzing the existing structural system.

The engineer may then compare structural demand and capacity under the applicable evaluation criteria and identify conditions requiring further investigation or potential strengthening. For retrofit projects, the final strategy must account for existing foundations, connections, architectural constraints, MEP systems, occupancy, constructability, and the owner's objectives. The evaluation methodology should be selected according to the specific building and applicable standards.

Do MEP systems require a structural engineer for seismic bracing?

MEP seismic bracing and structural engineering are related but distinct disciplines. Suspended ductwork, piping, conduit, cable trays, and other nonstructural components can require seismic restraint based on the applicable project requirements. The seismic restraint must ultimately transfer its forces to a supporting structure capable of resisting them.

That creates an important structural interface: MEP component → support → seismic restraint → attachment → primary structure. Structural engineering may therefore be necessary to evaluate attachment capacity, supporting members, anchorage, or other structural interfaces, depending on the project scope. The seismic bracing itself should not automatically be assumed to be adequate simply because the ordinary MEP support can carry the component's gravity load.

How can BIM and 3D CAD support seismic structural engineering?

BIM and 3D CAD can improve coordination by showing structural framing and seismic-related components in relation to architecture, equipment, ductwork, piping, cable trays, and other building systems. This can reveal conflicts before fabrication or installation.

For retrofit projects, digital modeling can also help organize existing-condition information and identify where new structural members, braces, anchors, or support frames can be installed. For engineered fabrication, coordinated CAD information can be carried into custom brackets, structural frames, strut assemblies, equipment supports, and other fabricated components.

The value of BIM is therefore not limited to visualization. When properly integrated into the engineering workflow, it can connect analysis, design documentation, coordination, fabrication, and construction more effectively.
