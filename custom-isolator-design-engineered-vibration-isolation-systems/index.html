Custom Isolator Design: Engineered Vibration Isolation Systems

anitaa September 30, 2026

Custom isolator design is an engineering process used when standard vibration mounts cannot adequately address the load, dynamic behavior, geometry, environmental conditions, or structural requirements of a specific piece of equipment. Rather than selecting an isolator solely by weight capacity, an engineered solution considers how equipment forces interact with stiffness, damping, natural frequency, mounting geometry, and the supporting structure.

This distinction becomes particularly important for rotating and reciprocating machinery, HVAC equipment, generators, pumps, compressors, marine engines, industrial process equipment, and sensitive equipment installed in commercial, healthcare, infrastructure, and mission-critical facilities. A mount may safely carry the static weight of equipment while still providing inadequate vibration isolation if its stiffness or dynamic characteristics are poorly matched to the operating frequency.

A custom isolator design may involve compression springs, wire rope, elastomeric materials, rubber-metal assemblies, specialized restraints, mounting plates, brackets, or a complete isolation base. The design can also account for unequal reactions at individual mounting points, limited clearances, unusual bolt patterns, temperature, corrosion, fatigue, shock, and seismic movement requirements.

The engineering pathway is therefore broader than the isolator itself:

Equipment → Operating Forces → Dynamic Characteristics → Isolation Requirements → Isolator Design → Mounting Interface → Structural Attachment → Validation

For U.S. construction and industrial projects, the isolation system may also need to coordinate with project specifications, equipment manufacturer requirements, adopted building codes, and applicable seismic provisions. The Sigma Source approaches custom isolation as an engineering and fabrication problem, connecting vibration-control analysis with structural interfaces, BIM/3D CAD coordination, and custom metal fabrication when the project requires it.

What Is Custom Isolator Design?

Custom isolator design means developing an isolation component or assembly around the actual characteristics of the equipment and its operating environment. The objective is not simply to manufacture a mount with unusual dimensions. The isolator must provide an appropriate mechanical response while carrying the equipment reliably and maintaining the required relationship between the equipment, support system, and structure.

Why Standard Isolators Are Not Always Enough

Catalog isolators are designed around defined ranges of loads, stiffness, dimensions, and operating conditions. They can be highly effective when the application falls within those parameters. Problems arise when equipment has unusual mounting points, substantial dynamic forces, unequal support reactions, restricted installation space, or environmental conditions outside the intended product range.

Custom vibration isolator design allows engineers to establish requirements first and select or develop the isolation mechanism afterward. Important variables can include static load per mount, dynamic loading, operating speed, excitation frequency, desired natural frequency, static deflection, damping, horizontal stability, displacement limits, and mounting geometry.

The complete assembly may include more than the isolating element. A custom equipment isolation mount can require steel plates, brackets, structural frames, attachment hardware, restraints, or flexible interfaces. Each component affects the behavior and constructability of the system.

For The Sigma Source, this creates a natural engineering-to-fabrication workflow: determine the performance requirements, develop the isolator and mounting interface, coordinate the assembly through 3D CAD, and fabricate supporting metal components when required. The result is an application-specific system rather than a generic mount adapted after the fact.

When Does Equipment Require a Custom Vibration Isolator?

A custom vibration isolator becomes relevant when the performance requirements of the equipment and support system do not align cleanly with available standard products. This can occur in both new construction and retrofit projects, and it is often driven by the interaction of mechanical requirements with physical constraints.

Nonstandard Loads and Mounting Geometry

Equipment does not always distribute its weight evenly across mounting points. A large machine may have an elevated center of gravity, offset components, or operating forces that change the reaction at individual supports. Designing every isolator for the same nominal percentage of total equipment weight can therefore produce an inaccurate load model.

Geometry is another common driver. Existing steel frames, concrete housekeeping pads, equipment curbs, and legacy mounting locations may dictate where an isolator can physically fit. A custom mount can accommodate nonstandard bolt patterns, limited clearance, unusual base dimensions, or required access for maintenance.

Dynamic and Operating Requirements

Rotating machinery introduces periodic excitation related to operating speed and harmonics. Reciprocating equipment can create more complex dynamic forces, while compressors, engines, pumps, fans, and generators can produce different combinations of vertical, horizontal, and torsional excitation.

Retrofit and Replacement Applications

Retrofit work creates additional challenges because the engineer may have to work with existing structural conditions rather than an idealized new installation. Field verification, existing drawings, equipment measurements, and actual support conditions become important design inputs.

When standard mounts cannot satisfy these combined requirements, custom isolator engineering provides a way to develop the isolation characteristics and physical interface around the real installation.

How Engineers Determine Custom Isolator Requirements

Successful custom isolator design starts with requirements definition rather than material selection. Engineers first establish what the equipment is doing, how it is supported, what forces it produces, and what level of vibration control is required.

Equipment Loads and Load Distribution

The equipment's total weight is only the beginning. Designers may need the reaction at each mounting point, operating loads, dynamic forces, center of gravity, and the relationship between the equipment base and support structure. These inputs determine whether each isolator can be treated as carrying an equal load or whether different isolator capacities are necessary.

Excitation Frequency and Operating Conditions

Operating speed is particularly important for rotating equipment. The excitation frequency must be evaluated against the isolation system's natural frequency. Startup, shutdown, variable-speed operation, harmonics, and transient conditions can also influence the required response.

The goal is not simply to make an isolator “soft.” The isolation system must have adequate load capacity and stability while producing the desired dynamic response. Excessive compliance can introduce unwanted movement, alignment problems, or clearance issues.

Environmental and Installation Requirements

Temperature, moisture, chemicals, ultraviolet exposure, marine conditions, contamination, and corrosion can change material performance over time. Installation requirements also matter. The design may need to accommodate limited overhead clearance, maintenance access, equipment alignment, flexible connections, and structural tolerances.

This information establishes the engineering envelope for the custom isolator before a spring, wire rope, elastomer, or hybrid assembly is selected.

Static Deflection, Stiffness, and Natural Frequency in Isolator Design

Static deflection, stiffness, natural frequency, damping, and transmissibility are interconnected variables in vibration isolation. Understanding their relationship is essential when developing engineered vibration isolators for equipment with significant operational excitation.

Static Deflection and Stiffness

Static deflection describes the displacement produced by the supported static load. For a simplified linear spring system, stiffness is related to load and deflection. Increasing compliance generally lowers the system's natural frequency, but that does not mean maximum deflection is always the design objective.

The isolator must still maintain equipment stability, allowable displacement, structural clearance, and appropriate load capacity. For systems with multiple supports, the design also has to account for load distribution and potential differences in reaction between individual mounts.

Natural Frequency and Resonance

An isolation system has a characteristic natural frequency determined by its effective stiffness and supported mass. When excitation occurs near that natural frequency, vibration response can increase significantly. Effective isolation generally requires the operating excitation to be sufficiently separated from the resonant region.

This is why an isolator selected only by static weight can perform poorly. Two mounts with identical load ratings may have very different stiffness and damping characteristics and therefore produce different dynamic results.

Transmissibility and Damping

Transmissibility describes how vibration is transferred through the isolation system. Damping affects the response near resonance and influences how the system behaves under dynamic excitation.

Custom vibration isolator design therefore considers the complete dynamic relationship rather than treating load capacity as the only selection criterion. The engineering objective is to achieve an appropriate balance between isolation, stability, displacement, durability, and installation constraints.

Selecting the Right Custom Isolator Type

The appropriate isolator type depends on the equipment's load, excitation, environmental conditions, required displacement, geometry, and performance objectives. No single isolation technology is appropriate for every application.

Custom Spring Isolators

Spring isolators can provide relatively low stiffness and substantial static deflection, making them useful for many HVAC and mechanical equipment applications. Custom spring mounts may be developed when equipment loads are unusual or when the mounting configuration requires specialized plates, housings, restraints, or dimensions.

Restrained or captive spring configurations can be considered where equipment movement must be controlled while maintaining an isolation function. The restraint system must be coordinated with the expected equipment movement rather than simply locking the isolator against motion.

Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed wire-rope elements to provide multidirectional compliance and energy dissipation. They can be useful in applications involving vibration, shock, impact, or demanding environmental conditions, including certain industrial, transportation, aerospace, and marine installations.

Their nonlinear behavior and geometry make application-specific evaluation important, particularly when displacement limits and multidirectional loads are significant.

Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Natural rubber, synthetic rubber, neoprene, EPDM, silicone, polyurethane, and other elastomeric compounds can provide different combinations of stiffness, damping, temperature resistance, and environmental durability. Rubber-metal isolators can integrate elastomeric elements with steel plates or other structural components.

Hybrid Assemblies

Some projects require a complete isolation assembly incorporating isolators, mounting plates, brackets, support frames, restraints, or custom structural interfaces. In these cases, the isolation element should be designed together with the hardware that transfers loads into the supporting structure.

Custom Isolator Design for HVAC, Industrial, and Rotating Equipment

Custom isolation systems serve a broad range of equipment because the source of vibration and the required performance can vary substantially from one application to another.

HVAC and Mechanical Equipment

Fans, pumps, air-handling units, chillers, compressors, boilers, cooling towers, and generators can transmit operational vibration into floors, roofs, equipment rooms, and connected building systems. Spring mounts, vibration isolation hangers, floor isolators, rubber mounts, and other systems may be considered based on equipment characteristics.

Isolation design must also account for connected piping, ductwork, electrical connections, and flexible components. A properly selected isolator can be undermined if rigid connections create unintended vibration paths around the isolation system.

Industrial Machinery

Manufacturing and process equipment can involve rotating or reciprocating forces, impact, cyclic loading, and demanding duty cycles. Custom machinery mounts may therefore require greater attention to fatigue, horizontal stability, shock, and long-term durability than a simple static-load calculation would provide.

Marine and Transportation Applications

Marine engines and shipboard machinery can combine high dynamic loading with restricted space, corrosion exposure, shock, and continuous operation. Custom marine engine mounts may require stainless or corrosion-resistant components, specialized geometry, and careful consideration of multidirectional loading.

Healthcare and Mission-Critical Facilities

Hospitals, laboratories, data centers, and other sensitive facilities can have stringent vibration requirements because equipment performance and occupant conditions may be affected by transmitted vibration. Isolation may need to be coordinated with seismic restraint and the facility's structural and MEP requirements.

The same engineering principles apply across these markets, but the design inputs and acceptance criteria must be established for the specific application.

Materials, Geometry, and Environmental Durability

Material selection is an engineering decision rather than a simple preference for rubber or metal. The material and geometry must maintain the required mechanical properties throughout the expected operating conditions and service life.

Elastomer Selection

Elastomeric materials can have substantially different properties. Temperature, chemical exposure, compression characteristics, aging, fatigue, and environmental conditions can affect performance. A material that works well in an indoor mechanical room may not be appropriate for a hot industrial environment or marine installation.

For custom rubber isolators, the compound, geometry, bonding, compression state, and supporting metal components all contribute to performance. The design should therefore consider the complete assembly rather than the nominal material designation alone.

Metal Components and Fabrication

Steel plates, brackets, housings, mounting frames, spring components, wire rope, machined parts, and attachment hardware may be required around the isolation element. Carbon steel can be appropriate for many indoor applications, while stainless steel or protected steel may be selected where corrosion exposure requires it.

The Sigma Source can connect custom isolator engineering with metal-processing capabilities such as laser or plasma cutting, forming, machining, welding, galvanizing, and powder coating when those processes are appropriate to the specified assembly.

Geometry and Fatigue

Geometry affects stress concentration, stiffness, load distribution, displacement, and fatigue. Cyclic loading is particularly important for machinery mounts because a component that survives a static load may experience substantially different stresses over millions of operating cycles.

Clearances, bolt-hole tolerances, alignment, access, and installation sequence should also be addressed during design. These considerations are where 3D CAD and fabrication coordination can materially improve constructability.

Seismic Compatibility and Structural Attachment

Vibration isolation and seismic restraint are related but distinct engineering functions. Confusing the two can produce an installation that controls operational vibration but does not adequately address seismic movement, or a restraint configuration that compromises the intended isolation performance.

Vibration Isolation vs. Seismic Restraint

Vibration isolation is intended to reduce the transmission of operational vibration between equipment and its supporting structure.

Seismic restraint is intended to limit movement and transfer applicable seismic forces through the equipment-support, connection, anchorage, and structural load path.

A system can incorporate both functions. For example, a restrained or captive isolator may permit controlled isolation movement while providing a defined limit on equipment displacement. The details must be engineered for the applicable loads and clearances.

Structural Interfaces and Anchorage

An isolation assembly ultimately interacts with a support condition such as a structural slab, housekeeping pad, steel frame, equipment rail, curb, or foundation. Anchors, welded connections, bolted connections, mounting plates, and structural members must be capable of transferring the applicable forces.

For building projects, ASCE 7 and the adopted IBC or CBC may become relevant when the equipment and its attachment fall within applicable seismic provisions. They should not be treated as universal vibration-isolator design standards.

For California healthcare facilities under HCAI jurisdiction, applicable HCAI requirements must also be considered. OSHPD remains relevant primarily as historical terminology in older healthcare documents and legacy references.

BIM, 3D CAD, and Engineering-to-Fabrication Workflow

Custom isolation projects often fail at interfaces rather than at the basic isolator concept. BIM and 3D CAD coordination can help identify those interfaces before fabrication or installation begins.

Digital Modeling of Custom Assemblies

A detailed model can represent equipment dimensions, mounting points, isolator locations, plates, brackets, support frames, restraints, bolt patterns, clearances, and surrounding structural members. This is particularly useful when a custom isolation assembly must fit within a congested mechanical room or retrofit installation.

Coordination With Structural and MEP Systems

Isolation systems must coexist with piping, ductwork, conduit, electrical connections, flexible connectors, housekeeping pads, steel framing, and maintenance access. Rigid connections that bypass an isolation system can create unintended vibration transmission paths, while inadequate clearances can restrict the movement needed for proper operation.

3D coordination gives engineers and contractors a common spatial reference for resolving these conditions.

Fabrication and Shop Documentation

Once the engineering geometry is established, CAD information can support fabrication drawings and manufacturing processes. Custom plates and brackets may be laser or plasma cut, formed, machined, and welded before receiving the specified protective finish.

The Sigma Source's combination of engineering, BIM/3D CAD, and custom metal fabrication supports this transition from design intent to physical assembly. Fabrication remains subordinate to the engineering requirements: the manufactured component must reproduce the intended geometry, load path, tolerances, and interfaces.

How to Plan a Custom Isolator Design Project

A custom isolation project becomes more efficient when the engineering team receives complete equipment information at the beginning. Missing data can force assumptions that later affect isolator selection, geometry, or performance.

Information to Provide the Engineer

Useful project information includes equipment weight, dimensions, center of gravity, mounting-hole pattern, load at each mounting point, operating speed, excitation frequency, dynamic forces, support conditions, available clearance, temperature, environmental exposure, and required vibration-performance criteria.

For retrofit work, field measurements and photographs can supplement existing drawings. Equipment manufacturer data, installation manuals, operating ranges, and OEM mounting requirements can also help establish accurate inputs.

Engineering and Fabrication Workflow

A typical workflow progresses from requirements definition to preliminary isolation analysis, isolator selection or development, load and dynamic calculations, CAD modeling, structural-interface review, design coordination, fabrication, inspection, and installation.

Depending on the application, prototype testing or performance validation may be appropriate. Material testing, compression testing, dynamic characterization, or application-specific testing can provide additional evidence when the required performance warrants it.

Coordination With the Project Team

Owners, OEMs, structural engineers, MEP contractors, architects, general contractors, facility managers, and procurement teams may each control important project information. Early coordination helps prevent a situation in which the isolator is correctly designed but cannot be installed because of an overlooked beam, pipe, curb, access requirement, or bolt pattern.

Conclusion: Engineering Custom Isolation Around the Real Application

Custom isolator design is fundamentally an application-engineering problem. The correct solution depends on the equipment's weight distribution, operating forces, excitation frequencies, stiffness requirements, damping characteristics, allowable movement, environmental exposure, mounting geometry, and structural interface. Selecting a mount by static load alone does not establish whether the system will provide effective vibration isolation.

A successful design begins with accurate equipment information and progresses through dynamic evaluation, isolator selection or development, mounting-interface design, structural coordination, CAD modeling, fabrication, inspection, and installation verification. Where necessary, the isolation system must also accommodate seismic restraint requirements without compromising its vibration-control function.

This approach is especially important for rotating machinery, HVAC equipment, industrial process equipment, generators, marine engines, healthcare systems, and other applications where transmitted vibration, shock, alignment, or equipment movement can affect performance or the surrounding facility.

Material, structural, welding, and testing requirements may introduce additional standards depending on the project.

FAQ: Custom Isolator Design

What is custom isolator design?

Custom isolator design is the engineering process of developing an isolation mount or complete isolation assembly around the requirements of a specific piece of equipment. Engineers may evaluate equipment weight, load distribution, operating speed, excitation frequency, dynamic forces, stiffness, static deflection, damping, natural frequency, geometry, environmental exposure, and structural attachment.

The result may be a spring isolator, wire rope isolator, elastomeric mount, rubber-metal assembly, restrained isolator, or specialized isolation base. The defining characteristic is that the design is developed around the application rather than selected solely from a standard catalog rating.

When should a custom vibration isolator be used?

A custom solution may be appropriate when a standard isolator cannot satisfy the required load, stiffness, deflection, geometry, environmental resistance, displacement limits, or vibration-performance criteria.

Custom design can also make sense for equipment with irregular mounting patterns, unequal support reactions, unusual center-of-gravity locations, restricted clearance, demanding temperatures, corrosive exposure, shock loading, or retrofit constraints. The decision should be based on engineering requirements rather than assuming that a custom isolator is automatically superior to a properly selected standard product.

How is a custom vibration isolator designed?

The process typically begins by collecting equipment and operating data. Engineers evaluate static and dynamic loads, mounting-point reactions, operating speed, excitation frequency, center of gravity, required isolation performance, environmental conditions, and available installation space.

Those requirements are translated into target stiffness, deflection, natural frequency, damping, geometry, and load capacity. The isolator and its mounting interface can then be modeled in CAD and reviewed against the supporting structure. Depending on the application, calculations, prototype testing, material testing, or performance validation may also be performed.

What information is required for custom isolator engineering?

Important inputs include equipment weight, dimensions, mounting locations, load at each mounting point, center of gravity, operating speed, excitation frequencies, dynamic loads, mounting-hole pattern, support conditions, available clearance, temperature, environmental exposure, and required performance.

For retrofit projects, field measurements can be particularly valuable because existing conditions may differ from design drawings. OEM equipment documentation, installation manuals, photographs, and existing support details can further improve the accuracy of the design basis.

What is the difference between static deflection and dynamic stiffness?

Static deflection is the displacement produced when an isolator supports a static load such as equipment weight. Dynamic stiffness describes how the isolator responds to changing or oscillating loads and can vary with material behavior, frequency, amplitude, and other conditions.

Both can be important to vibration isolation. An isolator can have sufficient static load capacity while still having inappropriate dynamic characteristics for the equipment's operating frequency. Custom vibration isolator design therefore evaluates the relationship between load, stiffness, natural frequency, damping, and excitation rather than relying on static capacity alone.

Are spring isolators suitable for custom equipment?

Spring isolators can be suitable for equipment requiring relatively low stiffness and substantial static deflection. They are commonly considered for HVAC and mechanical equipment, pumps, fans, chillers, generators, compressors, and certain industrial machinery.

Custom spring assemblies can accommodate unusual load distributions, dimensions, mounting plates, housings, or restraint arrangements. However, the spring is only one component of the system. The housing, mounting hardware, equipment interface, structural attachment, and any seismic restraint must also be evaluated as part of the complete installation.

When are wire rope isolators appropriate?

Wire rope isolators can be considered for applications involving vibration, shock, impact, or multidirectional movement. Their construction can be useful in certain industrial, marine, transportation, and other demanding environments.

Selection depends on the actual load, displacement, frequency range, mounting geometry, environmental exposure, and required performance. Their suitability should be established through application-specific engineering rather than selecting them simply because the equipment experiences severe service conditions.

Can a custom isolator provide seismic restraint?

Isolation and seismic restraint have different functions. Vibration isolation is intended to reduce operational vibration transmission, while seismic restraint limits movement and transfers applicable seismic forces through the equipment-support and structural load path.

A custom assembly can be engineered to coordinate both functions. Restrained or captive isolators, seismic brackets, structural frames, and separate restraint components may be used depending on the application. The final configuration must satisfy the applicable project criteria and should not compromise the intended vibration-isolation performance.

Do custom isolators need to meet ASCE 7?

ASCE 7 is not a universal vibration-isolator design standard. It may become relevant when an isolation assembly is incorporated into a building installation subject to applicable seismic provisions, including requirements affecting nonstructural components, equipment, supports, or attachments.

The project should also be reviewed against the adopted building code, jurisdictional amendments, project specifications, equipment requirements, and applicable structural standards. For California healthcare facilities within HCAI jurisdiction, applicable HCAI requirements must be incorporated into the project design.

What materials can be used for custom isolation mounts?

Material selection depends on load, temperature, fatigue, corrosion, chemical exposure, stiffness, damping, manufacturing requirements, and service life. Elastomeric systems may use natural rubber, synthetic rubber, neoprene, EPDM, silicone, polyurethane, or other compounds.

Metal components may use carbon steel, stainless steel, spring steel, aluminum, structural steel, or corrosion-protected materials. Galvanizing, powder coating, and other protective finishes can be considered when consistent with the environment and project specifications.

There is no universally correct material for every isolation application. The material and geometry should be selected together based on the required mechanical and environmental performance.
