Vibration Isolation: Engineering Guide to Systems, Mounts, and Applications

anitaa September 30, 2026

Modern commercial buildings, healthcare facilities, industrial plants, and infrastructure projects depend on mechanical and rotating equipment that can generate significant dynamic forces during operation. Pumps, fans, compressors, chillers, air handling units, generators, marine engines, and manufacturing machinery can transmit vibration into floors, structural members, equipment supports, and adjacent spaces when their dynamic behavior is not adequately addressed.

vibration isolation is an engineering approach used to reduce the transmission of these dynamic forces between equipment and its supporting structure. Properly selected isolation systems can help control structure-borne vibration, reduce mechanical noise transmission, protect sensitive equipment, and improve operating conditions. However, effective isolation is not simply a matter of placing a rubber pad beneath a machine. Isolator stiffness, static deflection, natural frequency, damping, operating speed, equipment weight, mounting geometry, structural conditions, and installation details all influence system performance.

For U.S. construction projects, vibration isolation may also need to be coordinated with seismic restraint, equipment anchorage, MEP connections, and applicable building-code requirements. This distinction becomes especially important in California healthcare facilities and other projects where structural and nonstructural component requirements must be addressed as part of the overall design.

The right isolation strategy therefore begins with understanding the source of vibration and the dynamic characteristics of the equipment. Engineers and contractors must then determine how the equipment interacts with the isolators and supporting structure. From spring and wire rope isolators to elastomeric mounts, acoustic hangers, floor isolation systems, and custom-fabricated equipment bases, different technologies serve different engineering requirements.

This guide examines the principles behind vibration isolation, major isolator technologies, selection criteria, HVAC and industrial applications, seismic coordination, common installation problems, and the engineering information required to develop a reliable isolation solution.

What Is Vibration Isolation?

Vibration isolation is the controlled separation of a vibrating or vibration-sensitive component from another structure through an engineered mechanical interface. In building applications, the objective is commonly to reduce the amount of equipment-generated vibration transmitted into the structure. In some specialized applications, the objective can be reversed: isolating sensitive equipment from vibration originating in the surrounding structure.

The isolation interface typically incorporates an elastic element, such as a steel spring, elastomer, rubber-metal assembly, or wire rope element. Depending on the application, damping may also be incorporated to control dynamic response. The resulting system changes the way forces travel between the equipment and its support.

This distinction is important because vibration isolation is not synonymous with vibration elimination. Every real mechanical system has some degree of dynamic response. The engineering objective is to manage that response so that vibration transmission remains compatible with equipment operation, structural conditions, occupant requirements, and project specifications.

Vibration Isolation vs. Vibration Control

Vibration control is the broader discipline encompassing isolation, damping, balancing, structural modifications, tuned systems, and other techniques used to manage vibration. Vibration isolation is one specific strategy within that discipline.

Vibration Isolation vs. Seismic Isolation

Seismic isolation is designed to modify a structure's response to earthquake excitation, often through bearings or sliding interfaces located within the structural load path. It is fundamentally different from isolating a pump, fan, chiller, or machine from operational vibration.

Vibration Isolation vs. Seismic Restraint

Seismic restraint is intended to limit unwanted movement during an earthquake. A vibration isolator, by contrast, intentionally permits controlled movement to reduce dynamic force transmission during normal operation. An isolated piece of equipment may therefore require additional seismic restraint or anchorage depending on the project's design requirements.

Understanding these distinctions prevents a common design error: assuming that an isolation mount automatically satisfies seismic requirements.

How Vibration Isolation Systems Work

A useful way to understand an isolation system is through the mass-spring-damper model. The equipment represents the supported mass, the isolator provides elastic stiffness, and damping influences how the system responds to dynamic excitation. Although actual equipment-support assemblies can be considerably more complex, this model provides a foundation for evaluating isolation behavior.

One of the most important variables is natural frequency. An isolation system has a characteristic frequency determined largely by its effective stiffness and supported mass. For a simplified single-degree-of-freedom system, natural frequency is related to stiffness and mass approximately as:

fn = (1/2π) √(k/m)

where fn is natural frequency, k is effective stiffness, and m is supported mass.

Natural Frequency

Isolation generally becomes increasingly effective when the excitation frequency is sufficiently above the isolator's natural frequency. This is why equipment operating speed and expected excitation frequencies must be considered during selection. Operating near resonance can produce undesirable amplification rather than useful isolation.

Static Deflection

Static deflection is the amount an isolator compresses under the supported static load. For an idealized spring system, greater static deflection generally corresponds to lower natural frequency. Consequently, static deflection is not merely a physical dimension; it is an important design parameter.

Damping

Damping dissipates vibratory energy and influences resonance behavior. Excessive or insufficient damping can affect the response differently depending on operating conditions. Engineers therefore need to consider the actual characteristics of the isolation technology rather than treating damping as an independent performance number.

Transmissibility

Transmissibility describes the relationship between the dynamic force or motion transmitted through the isolation system and the excitation applied to it. Isolation efficiency depends on frequency ratio, damping, system characteristics, and installation conditions.

In practice, these variables must be evaluated together. A mount with an attractive nominal load rating may not deliver the required isolation if its stiffness, deflection, or operating configuration is inappropriate for the equipment.

Why Mechanical Equipment Requires Vibration Isolation

Mechanical equipment produces vibration for many reasons. Rotating components can generate forces from imbalance, misalignment, bearing conditions, motor characteristics, aerodynamic effects, or periodic operating forces. Reciprocating machinery can introduce more complex dynamic loads. Even properly functioning equipment can generate enough vibration to affect a building if the transmission path is unfavorable.

Pumps and fans are common examples. A pump attached rigidly to a structural floor can transmit dynamic forces through its base and into the slab. A fan mounted to a structural frame can transfer vibration through supports and connected ductwork. Compressors and generators can present even more demanding dynamic conditions because of their operating characteristics and force levels.

HVAC equipment creates another important application category. Air handling units, chillers, cooling towers, rooftop units, boilers, pumps, and exhaust fans can produce both vibration and structure-borne noise. The issue is particularly important when mechanical equipment is located above occupied areas or near sensitive spaces.

Industrial environments introduce additional considerations. Manufacturing machinery may operate continuously at high speeds or under changing production loads. Aerospace and precision manufacturing environments can contain equipment that is itself highly sensitive to external vibration. Marine applications can expose isolation systems to vibration, shock, moisture, corrosion, and changing operating conditions.

The structural interface also matters. Vibration can travel through equipment bases, housekeeping pads, steel framing, concrete slabs, piping, ductwork, conduit, and other connected components. A well-selected isolator can therefore be undermined by a rigid bypass elsewhere in the assembly.

For this reason, vibration isolation should be considered as a system-level engineering problem. Equipment characteristics, isolation hardware, support structure, flexible connections, restraints, and installation details all contribute to the final dynamic behavior.

Types of Vibration Isolation Systems

Different isolation technologies provide different combinations of stiffness, deflection, damping, directional response, load capacity, durability, and restraint capability. Selection should therefore be based on engineering requirements rather than on a generic preference for one material or product type.

Spring Vibration Isolators

Steel spring isolators are commonly used where relatively high static deflection and low natural frequency are required. They can be configured for floor-mounted mechanical equipment, inertia bases, air handling units, pumps, fans, and other equipment.

Restrained or captive spring configurations can incorporate hardware intended to limit movement. This can be particularly relevant where isolation must be coordinated with lateral or vertical restraint requirements.

Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use helically wound cable assemblies formed into engineered support elements. Their mechanical response can accommodate motion in multiple directions, making them useful in applications involving vibration, shock, and challenging environmental conditions.

They are often considered for industrial, marine, transportation, and specialized equipment where compact geometry, durability, and multidirectional behavior are important.

Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Rubber and elastomeric mounts can be manufactured in configurations such as rubber-in-shear and rubber-metal assemblies. Their stiffness can be engineered according to the required load and movement characteristics.

Material selection matters because elastomer behavior can vary with temperature, frequency, aging, environmental exposure, and compound formulation. Neoprene, EPDM, natural rubber, and other compounds may have different suitability depending on the application.

Acoustic Hangers and Hanger Isolators

Suspended HVAC systems require isolation solutions that prevent vibration from traveling through hanger rods and structural attachments. Acoustic hangers and hanger isolators introduce compliant elements into the suspension system.

Correct installation is critical. A rigid connection, improperly loaded hanger, or direct contact between isolated and non-isolated components can create a bypass path.

Floor Vibration Isolators

Floor-mounted equipment may use spring, elastomeric, or other isolation systems between the equipment base and supporting floor or inertia base. The design must account for equipment weight distribution, center of gravity, operating forces, and available mounting locations.

Captive and Restrained Isolators

Captive systems combine isolation with controlled movement limits. They can be useful where equipment must remain supported while allowing normal vibratory movement, but they should not automatically be treated as a substitute for project-specific seismic engineering.

How to Select the Right Vibration Isolator

Selecting a vibration isolator begins with equipment data rather than product appearance. Engineers should establish the operating weight, equipment dimensions, center of gravity, mounting locations, operating speed, excitation characteristics, support conditions, and environmental requirements.

Equipment Weight and Operating Loads

The design weight should reflect the actual operating condition when appropriate. Equipment may have substantially different loads when tanks are filled, systems are charged, fluids are circulating, or production loads are present. Uneven load distribution can also cause individual mounts to carry significantly different forces.

Operating Speed and Excitation Frequency

Rotational speed is commonly expressed in revolutions per minute, while vibration analysis may use frequency in hertz. Harmonics and other excitation frequencies should also be considered where relevant. The isolator system should be evaluated in relation to these excitation frequencies.

Required Static Deflection and Target Natural Frequency

Static deflection provides an important connection between equipment load, isolator stiffness, and natural frequency. The desired isolation performance should be established before choosing the mount configuration.

Equipment Center of Gravity

A high or offset center of gravity can influence stability and load distribution. Mount locations should provide adequate support without creating excessive rocking or uneven deflection.

Mounting Configuration

The number, location, orientation, and individual capacity of isolators affect system behavior. Four identical mounts are not automatically equivalent to a deliberately engineered multi-point support system.

Environmental Conditions

Temperature, moisture, chemicals, corrosion, salt exposure, UV exposure, and other environmental conditions can affect materials and finishes. Marine and industrial environments may require different material and coating strategies than conditioned commercial interiors.

Seismic Requirements

In seismic regions, the isolation design must be coordinated with applicable structural and nonstructural component requirements. The final assembly may require separate restraints, anchors, brackets, or other provisions.

Manufacturer Data and Project Specifications

Manufacturer performance data, equipment submittals, structural information, and project specifications should be reviewed together. Isolation should be selected from verified load and performance information rather than relying solely on generic equipment categories.

Vibration Isolation for HVAC and MEP Equipment

HVAC and MEP systems are among the most common building applications for vibration isolation because mechanical equipment is frequently connected to both the structure and extensive distribution systems. An isolation design that addresses only the equipment base can therefore be incomplete.

Air handling units may be mounted on spring or elastomeric isolators depending on equipment characteristics, support configuration, and project requirements. Chillers and pumps can require more substantial isolation assemblies because of their operating mass and dynamic behavior. Fans and exhaust equipment can generate significant rotating vibration, particularly when operating at elevated speeds.

Piping and ductwork introduce additional transmission paths. If an isolated piece of equipment is connected to rigid piping without appropriate flexibility, the connection can transfer vibration around the isolators. Similar issues can occur with ductwork, conduit, electrical connections, and other services.

Suspended equipment presents another challenge. HVAC units suspended from structural framing can transmit vibration through hanger rods and attachments. Acoustic hanger assemblies can introduce isolation into this load path while maintaining the required support configuration.

The supporting structure also needs consideration. An isolator does not eliminate the forces associated with the equipment; it changes their transmission characteristics. Floor capacity, support framing, housekeeping pads, inertia bases, and local structural conditions may all influence the installation.

For MEP contractors, coordination is particularly important. Equipment schedules should identify operating weights and mounting requirements early enough for structural and architectural coordination. For engineers, isolation requirements should be communicated clearly in drawings and specifications so procurement and installation teams understand the intended configuration.

A successful HVAC vibration-control strategy therefore extends from the equipment through the isolator, base, structure, piping, ductwork, and other interfaces. Each component must preserve the intended isolation path.

Vibration Isolation Design and Engineering Considerations

Engineering vibration isolation requires more than matching a mount to a nominal equipment weight. The objective is to create a predictable mechanical interface that addresses dynamic behavior while satisfying structural, operational, environmental, and project requirements.

Dynamic load evaluation is an important starting point. The designer needs to understand what forces the equipment generates, at which frequencies they occur, and how those forces interact with the support structure. Operating speed, harmonics, startup and shutdown conditions, and variable-speed operation may all affect the response.

Isolation efficiency should then be considered in relation to the frequency ratio between equipment excitation and isolation-system natural frequency. The system must operate away from undesirable resonance conditions, while damping characteristics influence response around resonance.

Stiffness and deflection also affect stability. An isolator that is too stiff may transmit excessive dynamic force. One that is too flexible may create excessive movement or equipment instability. Uneven loading can further complicate the response by causing different mounts to deflect differently.

The structural interface is equally important. Engineers may need to evaluate the supporting slab, steel frame, inertia base, housekeeping pad, or other support element. The objective is not simply to specify an isolator but to ensure the complete support assembly behaves as intended.

Installation details deserve the same attention. Isolation can be compromised when piping, conduit, ductwork, equipment guards, housekeeping pads, or structural attachments create unintended rigid paths. Clearance around moving components and restraint hardware must also be maintained.

Vibration Isolation and Seismic Requirements in U.S. Projects

Vibration isolation and seismic protection often intersect on U.S. construction projects, but they should be engineered as distinct functions. A vibration isolator is intended to provide controlled compliance during equipment operation. Seismic restraint is intended to limit potentially damaging movement during an earthquake.

The applicable requirements depend on the building, location, occupancy, equipment, structural system, and project-specific design criteria. The International Building Code (IBC) provides a framework used by many U.S. jurisdictions, while the California Building Code (CBC) establishes requirements applicable to California projects. ASCE 7 contains structural design provisions that can be relevant to seismic forces and nonstructural components.

Healthcare facilities can involve additional requirements and review considerations through the California Department of Health Care Access and Information (HCAI), formerly associated with the Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development (OSHPD). Equipment isolation and restraint details should therefore be coordinated with the applicable healthcare-facility requirements and project documentation.

A common engineering challenge occurs when equipment must remain isolated during normal operation but also requires restraint during a seismic event. Restrained spring isolators, seismic snubbers, brackets, anchors, and other restraint assemblies may be incorporated depending on the engineered design.

The interaction between these systems requires careful attention to clearances, attachment points, load paths, and movement limits. A restraint installed incorrectly can create a rigid bypass during normal operation, reducing the effectiveness of the isolation system.

For this reason, seismic calculations and vibration-isolation design should not be treated as unrelated procurement tasks. Structural engineers, MEP engineers, equipment manufacturers, contractors, and installers need coordinated information describing both operating requirements and seismic conditions.

For California and healthcare projects, documentation, calculations, submittals, and approval requirements should be verified against the governing project criteria rather than relying on a generic statement of compliance.

Common Vibration Isolation Design and Installation Problems

Many vibration problems arise not because isolation technology is inherently inadequate, but because the selected system does not match the actual equipment or installation conditions.

Incorrect Isolator Selection

Selecting an isolator solely from a nominal equipment weight can produce uneven loading, inappropriate stiffness, or insufficient deflection. The actual operating condition and individual mount loads should be evaluated.

Insufficient or Excessive Deflection

Insufficient deflection can result in a system that remains relatively stiff and transmits more vibration than intended. Excessive deflection can introduce stability, movement, clearance, or alignment problems.

Rigid Bypass Paths

A rigid bypass is one of the most common practical concerns. Piping, ductwork, electrical connections, structural attachments, or other components can unintentionally bridge an isolation interface.

Incorrect Equipment Loading

Equipment weight can change depending on operating fluids, accessories, connected components, or process conditions. Procurement data should be reconciled with the actual installation condition.

Improper Installation

An isolator that is damaged, misaligned, incorrectly loaded, or installed without required clearances may not perform according to its design characteristics. Installation instructions and approved details should be followed.

Inadequate Seismic Restraint Coordination

Adding seismic restraints after installation can create conflicts with vibration isolation. Restraints should be coordinated during design so that required movement and load paths are understood.

Flexible Connection Problems

Flexible connectors must be compatible with the expected movement and service conditions. A connector that is too stiff, improperly aligned, or mechanically short-circuited can compromise the intended isolation path.

Structural Transmission Paths

Even when the equipment mount is correctly selected, vibration can travel through adjacent structural members or connected services. Troubleshooting should therefore examine the complete transmission path rather than focusing only on the isolator.

A systematic review of equipment data, mounting conditions, operating frequencies, structural interfaces, connected services, and installation details provides a stronger basis for diagnosing persistent vibration than replacing mounts without identifying the underlying mechanism.

Custom Vibration Isolation Systems and Fabricated Components

Standard isolation components are appropriate for many applications, but project geometry, equipment configuration, load distribution, environmental exposure, or structural requirements can create conditions where custom fabrication is useful.

Custom equipment bases and inertia bases can provide a controlled interface between isolation hardware and irregular or heavy equipment. Fabricated mounting frames can also distribute equipment loads to predetermined isolator locations when the equipment's factory base does not align with the available structural support.

Custom brackets and support assemblies may be required when existing steel, concrete, or MEP configurations limit standard mounting options. Fabrication can involve carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum, structural steel, or sheet metal depending on strength, weight, corrosion exposure, finish, and project specifications.

Metal processing can also be integrated into the fabrication workflow. Plasma cutting and laser cutting can produce precise components, while welding, forming, stamping, and machining support different assembly requirements. Galvanizing or powder coating can provide specified surface finishes where appropriate.

BIM and 3D CAD modeling can support coordination between isolation assemblies, equipment, structural members, and surrounding MEP systems. This can be particularly useful in congested mechanical rooms or retrofit environments where available clearances must be verified before fabrication.

For marine and industrial applications, environmental conditions can have a greater influence on material selection. Stainless steel, protective coatings, specialized elastomers, and wire rope assemblies may be considered according to the operating environment.

The engineering objective remains the same regardless of whether the solution is standard or custom: the fabricated assembly must provide the required load path, accommodate the isolation hardware, maintain equipment stability, and integrate with the surrounding structure and services. Custom fabrication should therefore follow engineering requirements rather than serve as a substitute for them.

How Engineers and Contractors Should Specify Vibration Isolation

A clear specification gives procurement and installation teams the information needed to reproduce the intended engineering design. Ambiguous requirements can lead to substitutions that appear similar but have materially different stiffness, deflection, or restraint characteristics.

Equipment information should include manufacturer and model when available, operating weight, dimensions, center of gravity, operating speed, equipment mounting locations, and any variable operating conditions. Pumps, fans, compressors, chillers, and other rotating equipment should be evaluated according to their actual operating characteristics.

Isolation requirements should identify the intended isolation technology, required static deflection or performance criteria, mounting arrangement, load per isolator, and any applicable environmental requirements. Where proprietary products are specified, equivalent-product provisions should be evaluated carefully rather than assuming that nominal load capacity establishes equivalency.

Structural information is equally important. The supporting slab, steel framing, equipment base, housekeeping pad, or inertia base should be compatible with the equipment loads and isolation configuration. Anchor locations and structural attachments should be coordinated with the responsible engineer.

Seismic requirements should be documented separately and coordinated with the vibration isolation system. Applicable IBC, CBC, ASCE 7, HCAI requirements, local amendments, and project specifications should be reviewed according to the project's jurisdiction and occupancy.

Material and finish requirements should address corrosion exposure and service conditions. Stainless steel, galvanized steel, powder-coated steel, or other specified finishes may be appropriate depending on the application.

Finally, submittals should contain enough technical information for review. Load tables, dimensions, stiffness or deflection data, installation details, restraint information, and applicable calculations can help engineers verify that the proposed assembly corresponds to the design intent.

Vibration Isolation for Commercial, Healthcare, and Industrial Facilities

The required vibration-control strategy varies substantially by facility type. In commercial buildings, the primary concern may be limiting vibration and structure-borne noise from HVAC equipment so that occupants are not disturbed. Mechanical rooms located above offices, residential areas, conference spaces, or other occupied zones often require careful treatment of equipment and connected services.

Healthcare facilities can impose more demanding coordination requirements. Hospitals may contain imaging systems, laboratory equipment, operating environments, patient-care areas, and sensitive building services where vibration performance matters. Mechanical equipment may also need to satisfy applicable healthcare construction and seismic requirements. HCAI projects require particular attention to the governing California requirements and approval process.

Data centers and mission-critical facilities can place emphasis on equipment reliability, continuous operation, structural coordination, and maintenance access. Mechanical systems may include large pumps, chillers, cooling equipment, generators, and other rotating machinery whose dynamic behavior must be coordinated with the facility's operational objectives.

Manufacturing plants introduce process-specific considerations. Equipment can operate continuously, change speed, experience variable production loads, or generate substantial dynamic forces. Isolation systems may therefore need to account for machinery behavior beyond the conditions normally encountered in commercial HVAC installations.

Marine and aerospace applications can involve compact equipment footprints, harsh environments, shock or dynamic loading, and demanding material requirements. Wire rope isolators, elastomeric systems, spring assemblies, and custom metal components may all have roles depending on the equipment and operating environment.

Across these applications, the common principle is system integration. Vibration isolation should be coordinated with the equipment, structure, MEP connections, seismic requirements, materials, fabrication, and installation conditions.

Conclusion: Engineering Vibration Isolation as a Complete System

Effective vibration isolation begins with understanding the dynamic relationship between equipment, isolators, supporting structures, and connected systems. The appropriate solution depends on more than equipment weight. Operating speed, excitation frequency, static deflection, natural frequency, damping, stiffness, center of gravity, mounting geometry, environmental conditions, and structural interfaces all influence the final result.

Spring vibration isolators, wire rope isolators, rubber and elastomeric mounts, acoustic hangers, floor isolation systems, and captive configurations each address different engineering conditions. Choosing among them requires verified equipment data and a clear understanding of the performance requirements. A mount that works appropriately for one pump, fan, or air handling unit cannot automatically be assumed to be suitable for another.

Installation and coordination are equally important. Rigid bypass paths through piping, ductwork, conduit, structural attachments, or other components can undermine an otherwise appropriate isolation design. Seismic restraint must also be considered separately and coordinated with the isolation system when required by the project.

For U.S. construction projects, applicable requirements may include the IBC, CBC, ASCE 7, jurisdictional amendments, project specifications, and, for applicable California healthcare facilities, HCAI requirements. Compliance should be evaluated according to the specific project rather than treated as a generic product attribute.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vibration Isolation

What is vibration isolation?

Vibration isolation is an engineering method for reducing the transmission of mechanical vibration between equipment and its supporting structure, or between a vibrating environment and sensitive equipment. Isolation is typically achieved through compliant elements such as springs, elastomers, rubber-metal assemblies, or wire rope systems.

The isolator changes the mechanical relationship between the supported mass and the structure. Proper performance depends on factors such as natural frequency, excitation frequency, static deflection, damping, stiffness, equipment weight, and mounting configuration. Isolation does not necessarily eliminate vibration; it manages how vibratory motion and forces are transmitted.

How does a vibration isolator work?

A vibration isolator can be understood through a mass-spring-damper model. The equipment acts as the supported mass, the isolator provides stiffness, and damping controls energy dissipation. When the system's natural frequency is sufficiently separated from the equipment's excitation frequency, the transmission of vibration can be reduced.

The actual behavior depends on the complete assembly. Equipment weight, mount stiffness, static deflection, damping, operating speed, and installation geometry all influence performance. This is why selecting a mount based only on a nominal weight rating is not sufficient for many engineering applications.

What type of vibration isolator is best for HVAC equipment?

There is no universal best isolator for every HVAC application. Selection depends on equipment type, operating weight, speed, mounting arrangement, required static deflection, structural support, available space, environmental conditions, and seismic requirements.

Spring isolators may be appropriate for equipment requiring greater deflection and lower natural frequency. Elastomeric mounts can be useful for applications with different load and stiffness requirements, while acoustic hangers are used for suspended equipment. Restrained configurations may be considered when controlled movement and seismic coordination are required.

The equipment manufacturer's data and project specifications should be evaluated before selecting a specific configuration.

What is the difference between spring and rubber vibration isolators?

Spring isolators and rubber or elastomeric isolators use different mechanical mechanisms and can have significantly different stiffness and deflection characteristics.

Steel springs can provide relatively high static deflection and low natural frequencies, which can make them suitable for certain large mechanical equipment applications. Rubber and elastomeric mounts rely on the deformation of an elastomeric compound and can provide useful damping and compact mounting configurations.

Elastomer properties can vary with compound formulation, temperature, frequency, aging, and environmental exposure. Therefore, the choice should be based on the required dynamic characteristics and service conditions rather than material preference alone.

When should wire rope isolators be used?

Wire rope isolators can be considered when an application benefits from their multidirectional mechanical response, compact construction, durability, or environmental resistance. They are used in a range of industrial, marine, transportation, and specialized equipment applications.

Their suitability depends on the equipment mass, expected displacement, excitation characteristics, environmental conditions, mounting geometry, and required load capacity. The wire rope assembly should be selected according to verified performance data rather than simply because it is a wire rope design.

How is static deflection related to vibration isolation?

Static deflection describes how much an isolator moves under the supported static load. It is closely related to isolator stiffness and therefore influences natural frequency.

For an idealized spring-mass system, a more flexible isolator generally produces greater static deflection and a lower natural frequency. The relationship is important because effective isolation often requires the system's natural frequency to be sufficiently separated from the equipment's excitation frequency.

Static deflection should nevertheless be evaluated together with stability, movement, equipment alignment, clearance, and load distribution. More deflection is not automatically better if the resulting system creates unacceptable movement or support conditions.

Does vibration isolation provide seismic protection?

Not automatically. Vibration isolation and seismic restraint have different purposes.

An isolator intentionally introduces compliance so that equipment-generated vibration is less readily transmitted into the structure. Seismic restraint, by contrast, limits equipment movement caused by earthquake excitation. An isolated piece of mechanical equipment may therefore require additional seismic restraint, anchorage, or other engineered provisions.

On projects subject to seismic requirements, the isolation system and seismic restraint should be coordinated during design. Applicable requirements can depend on building location, occupancy, equipment characteristics, structural system, and governing codes.

What standards apply to vibration isolation in U.S. construction?

Vibration isolation requirements can be influenced by project specifications, equipment requirements, structural design criteria, and applicable building and seismic provisions. Depending on the project, relevant frameworks can include the International Building Code, California Building Code, and ASCE 7.

For applicable California healthcare facilities, HCAI requirements and project-specific approval procedures may also be relevant. The former OSHPD terminology is still encountered in specifications and industry documentation, while HCAI is the current agency designation.

A project should be evaluated according to the governing jurisdiction, adopted code edition, local amendments, occupancy, and specific engineering requirements. A manufacturer's general statement should not replace project-specific code review.

Can vibration isolators be custom fabricated?

Yes. Custom isolation-related assemblies can include equipment bases, inertia bases, mounting frames, structural brackets, support plates, custom strut configurations, and other fabricated components.

Custom fabrication can be useful when equipment geometry does not match standard mounting arrangements, structural supports are irregular, space is restricted, or environmental requirements call for specific materials or finishes.

Depending on the application, fabrication may use carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum, structural steel, or sheet metal. Processes such as laser cutting, plasma cutting, welding, forming, machining, galvanizing, and powder coating can be incorporated according to the engineering and project requirements.

How do you calculate the required vibration isolator?

A vibration-isolation design typically begins with equipment operating data. Important inputs can include total operating weight, equipment dimensions, center of gravity, number and location of mounts, operating speed, excitation frequency, desired natural frequency, static deflection, stiffness, damping characteristics, and support conditions.

The designer should also evaluate whether the equipment experiences variable loads, startup forces, shutdown conditions, or other dynamic conditions. Structural support and connected piping or ductwork may need to be considered as part of the overall system.

For projects in seismic regions, seismic restraint and anchorage requirements should be evaluated separately and coordinated with the isolation design.

Can vibration isolation reduce structure-borne noise?

Properly designed vibration isolation can reduce the transmission of mechanical vibration into building structures, which can in turn reduce one important pathway for structure-borne noise.

However, vibration isolation does not address every source of acoustic transmission. Airborne sound, equipment casing noise, duct-borne noise, piping noise, and other transmission paths may require additional engineering measures.

The most effective approach is therefore to identify the actual transmission path. If vibration is traveling from a fan through its supports and ductwork into the building structure, the isolation system and flexible connections may need to be evaluated together.

What information should contractors provide when ordering vibration isolation equipment?

Contractors should provide as much verified equipment information as possible. This commonly includes the equipment manufacturer and model, operating weight, dimensions, center of gravity, operating speed, mounting locations, support arrangement, and environmental conditions.

For rotating equipment, operating speed and dynamic characteristics are particularly important. For HVAC equipment, connected piping, ductwork, electrical services, and suspended support conditions should also be considered.

Project-specific seismic requirements, structural support information, required materials or coatings, and approved specifications should be included where applicable. Providing this information early helps engineers and manufacturers select an isolation configuration that corresponds to the actual installation rather than relying on assumptions.

How can vibration isolation be integrated with seismic restraint?

Integration begins by treating normal operating vibration and seismic movement as related but distinct design conditions. The isolator must provide the required compliance during equipment operation while the overall assembly must satisfy applicable seismic movement and restraint requirements.

Depending on the project, this may involve restrained springs, seismic snubbers, brackets, anchors, structural attachments, or other engineered restraint components. Clearances and movement limits must be coordinated so the restraint system does not unintentionally create a rigid vibration path during normal operation.

The structural attachment also matters. Seismic forces must ultimately transfer through an appropriate load path into the supporting structure. Consequently, isolation, restraint, anchorage, and structural design should be coordinated rather than specified independently.
