Vibration Mounting and Controls: Engineering Guide to Vibration Isolation Systems

anitaa September 30, 2026

Mechanical equipment rarely operates without generating some degree of dynamic force. Motors rotate, fans produce aerodynamic forces, pumps create hydraulic excitation, compressors generate reciprocating or rotating loads, and generators transmit periodic forces into their supports. When those forces reach a building structure, they can become more than an equipment issue. They may produce floor vibration, structure-borne noise, occupant discomfort, equipment misalignment, fatigue concerns, or interference with sensitive operations.

vibration mounting and controls provide an engineered interface between dynamic equipment and the structure supporting it. Rather than treating a mount as a simple rubber pad or spring, effective vibration control considers the complete mechanical system: equipment mass, operating speed, excitation frequency, natural frequency, static deflection, damping, dynamic stiffness, mounting geometry, structural stiffness, and the available vibration-transmission paths.

This distinction is particularly important in U.S. commercial, industrial, healthcare, and infrastructure projects. A vibration isolation system for an air handling unit does not necessarily have the same design requirements as one supporting a high-speed pump, precision manufacturing machine, marine engine, or generator. Equipment weight can vary substantially between operating and shutdown conditions, while variable-speed drives can change excitation frequencies throughout normal operation.

The design also has to coexist with other project requirements. Seismic restraint, structural support, flexible piping and duct connections, equipment anchorage, maintenance access, and manufacturer requirements can all affect the final mounting arrangement. In California healthcare facilities, applicable HCAI requirements can add another layer of coordination.

A technically sound approach therefore begins with the source of vibration and follows its transmission path through the equipment support and building structure. The objective is not simply to install a mount, but to develop a coordinated system that provides the required vibration performance while maintaining equipment stability, structural integrity, constructability, and applicable seismic protection.

What Are Vibration Mounting and Controls?

Vibration mounting is the engineered use of mounts, isolators, bases, hangers, support frames, and related components to control how dynamic forces move between equipment and its supporting structure. Vibration control is the broader engineering discipline that addresses the source, transmission path, receiver, and dynamic response of the system.

A vibration isolator is intended to reduce force transmission between a vibrating machine and its support. A damping device, by comparison, dissipates energy and can help control resonance or transient motion. Structural vibration control may involve modifying the stiffness, mass, damping, or geometry of a supporting structure. Acoustic isolation focuses more specifically on limiting the transmission of sound and structure-borne noise. These approaches can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

The basic transmission path can be understood as:

Equipment → Mount → Equipment Base or Support → Structural System → Surrounding Space

Each part influences the final result. For example, a properly selected spring isolator may perform as intended when installed beneath a rigid equipment base, yet its effectiveness can be compromised if rigid piping, conduit, ductwork, or structural attachments create parallel transmission paths.

Mounting design also depends on the equipment itself. Engineers need to consider operating weight, center of gravity, mounting-point locations, rotational speed, startup and shutdown conditions, excitation forces, and the support structure's characteristics. A four-point mounting arrangement with an evenly distributed static load presents a different problem from an irregularly loaded machine with a high center of gravity.

This is why vibration mounts should not be selected solely from a nominal equipment weight. The mount must be compatible with the dynamic behavior of the complete system.

For commercial and industrial projects, the performance criteria may come from project specifications, owner requirements, equipment manufacturers, facility criteria, or engineering analysis. In sensitive facilities such as hospitals, laboratories, research spaces, and precision manufacturing environments, vibration requirements can be considerably more demanding than ordinary occupant-comfort considerations.

How Vibration Travels Through Mechanical Equipment and Buildings

Mechanical vibration can reach surrounding spaces through several interconnected paths. The most direct path occurs when equipment forces pass through its mounting points into a concrete slab, steel frame, equipment platform, or foundation. Once introduced into the structure, vibration can propagate through floors, beams, columns, walls, piping, and other connected components.

This is commonly described as structure-borne vibration. Airborne sound is a different transmission mechanism, although the two can interact. A vibrating piece of equipment can radiate acoustic energy into the surrounding air while simultaneously transmitting dynamic forces through its structural supports.

Common vibration sources include motors, pumps, fans, chillers, cooling towers, compressors, boilers, generators, air handling units, manufacturing equipment, process machinery, and marine engines. Rotating equipment is especially sensitive to operating speed and imbalance. A machine running at 1,800 RPM, for example, has a fundamental rotational frequency of approximately 30 Hz, before considering harmonics and other excitation components.

Resonance becomes a major concern when an excitation frequency approaches a natural frequency of the equipment-support system or another part of the structure. Instead of simply passing through the system, the dynamic response can become amplified. This is one reason an apparently rigid and robust mounting arrangement can still produce undesirable vibration.

The structure itself matters. A heavy concrete foundation, elevated steel platform, long-span floor, rooftop curb, or lightweight equipment frame can respond differently to the same excitation force. Floor stiffness, span, mass distribution, connection details, and nearby equipment all influence the resulting response.

Vibration control therefore requires consideration of both the source and the transmission path. Isolating the machine without addressing connected piping or ductwork may leave a significant bypass route. Conversely, modifying a structural support without understanding equipment excitation may fail to address the actual source.

For this reason, vibration mounting and controls should be treated as a system-level engineering problem rather than a component-selection exercise.

Engineering Principles Behind Vibration Mounting Systems

The effectiveness of an isolation system depends heavily on the relationship between the equipment's excitation frequency and the natural frequency of the isolated equipment-support system.

Natural Frequency and Excitation Frequency

An isolation system has a natural frequency determined primarily by its effective stiffness and supported mass. When the excitation frequency is sufficiently above the isolation system's natural frequency, force transmissibility can decrease substantially. When the excitation frequency is close to the natural frequency, resonance can instead produce a significant response.

This relationship is often expressed through a frequency ratio:

Frequency Ratio = Excitation Frequency / Natural Frequency

The design objective is generally to establish an appropriate separation between operating excitation frequencies and the isolator's natural frequency, while maintaining equipment stability and acceptable movement.

Static Deflection and Spring Rate

For spring-supported systems, static deflection is closely related to stiffness and natural frequency. A softer spring generally provides greater deflection under the same load and can produce a lower natural frequency. That can be beneficial for isolating low-frequency vibration, but the spring still has to support the equipment reliably.

The design must account for actual loading at each mounting point rather than dividing total equipment weight equally by assumption. Center-of-gravity location, frame geometry, accessories, fluid conditions, and support reactions can create uneven loads.

Transmissibility and Isolation Efficiency

Transmissibility describes how dynamic excitation is transferred through an isolation system. Engineers may use transmissibility relationships and manufacturer performance data to evaluate expected isolation behavior across the relevant frequency range.

Isolation efficiency cannot be judged from static load capacity alone. Two mounts that support the same equipment weight can have very different dynamic characteristics.

Damping and Dynamic Stiffness

Damping can reduce resonance amplification and control movement during transient conditions. Dynamic stiffness, meanwhile, describes how an isolator responds under dynamic loading and may differ from its static stiffness.

The appropriate balance depends on the application. A precision machine, HVAC fan, industrial compressor, and marine engine may require very different combinations of stiffness, damping, displacement control, and environmental durability.

The resulting engineering calculation should consider the equipment, isolator, base, support structure, and operating conditions as one connected dynamic system.

Types of Vibration Mounts and Isolation Systems

Different vibration sources require different isolation technologies. The correct selection depends on loading, frequency, displacement, environment, stability, installation arrangement, and performance criteria.

Spring Vibration Isolators

Spring vibration isolators are commonly used where relatively low natural frequencies and substantial static deflection are required. Open springs, restrained springs, housed springs, and captive configurations can address different combinations of vertical loading, lateral movement, equipment stability, and seismic requirements.

Spring selection requires more than matching a catalog load range. Engineers should evaluate the actual load carried by each spring, required deflection, operating frequency, equipment geometry, and movement limits.

Rubber and Elastomeric Isolators

Rubber-in-shear mounts, rubber-metal isolators, neoprene mounts, and elastomeric pads provide compact isolation solutions for many mechanical and industrial applications. Their performance depends on material properties, geometry, hardness, temperature, loading, aging, and environmental exposure.

Elastomeric systems can be particularly useful when space is limited or when a compact mounting arrangement is desirable. Material selection should reflect actual service conditions rather than relying on a generic rubber designation.

Wire Rope Isolators

Wire rope isolators use formed metallic cable elements to provide multidirectional flexibility and energy dissipation. They can be useful in industrial, marine, aerospace, electronics, and equipment applications where environmental durability, multidirectional response, or resistance to harsh conditions is important.

Acoustic Hangers and Floor Isolation

Suspended HVAC equipment and piping may use spring hangers or acoustic hangers to interrupt vibration transmission through building framing. Floor vibration isolators, elastomeric pads, isolation rails, and equipment bases can serve similar functions for floor-mounted equipment.

In some installations, an isolation system is combined with an inertia base or structural support frame. The base can improve equipment stability and distribute loads while working with the selected isolators.

The important principle is that each technology should be selected based on the dynamic requirements of the application rather than by assuming that one type of mount is universally appropriate.

How to Select the Right Vibration Mount

Vibration mount selection begins with accurate equipment information. At minimum, the engineering team should establish equipment operating weight, mounting-point geometry, center of gravity, operating speed, excitation characteristics, support conditions, and environmental requirements.

Equipment Loading and Geometry

A mount must carry the expected load at its individual support location. Uneven loading can occur when motors, gearboxes, fans, pumps, or other heavy components are concentrated toward one end of the equipment.

Mount locations also affect rotational stability. A high center of gravity combined with closely spaced mounts can produce different overturning behavior than a low-profile machine with widely distributed supports.

Operating Speed

RPM should be evaluated together with the equipment's excitation spectrum. The fundamental rotational frequency is not necessarily the only relevant frequency. Blade-pass frequencies, gear-mesh frequencies, reciprocating forces, harmonics, and variable-speed operation can all influence the dynamic response.

Environmental Conditions

Temperature, moisture, chemicals, UV exposure, marine environments, and corrosion can affect material selection. Rubber and elastomeric compounds should be appropriate for the service environment, while steel components may require stainless steel, galvanizing, powder coating, or another protective system.

Stability and Movement

Isolation requires flexibility, but uncontrolled movement is not acceptable. Engineers may need to consider lateral displacement, equipment alignment, overturning, startup forces, shutdown conditions, and restraint requirements.

Flexible connectors must also be coordinated carefully. Piping or ductwork that is connected rigidly to isolated equipment can create a mechanical bridge that bypasses the intended isolation system.

For complex installations, engineered selection can include calculated support reactions, natural-frequency evaluation, manufacturer data, structural analysis, and coordinated drawings.

Vibration Mounting for HVAC, Industrial, and Sensitive Equipment

HVAC systems are among the most common applications for vibration mounting and controls. Pumps, fans, chillers, cooling towers, air handling units, compressors, and generators can all introduce dynamic forces into building structures.

A centrifugal pump may require isolation at its base while also requiring flexible piping connections to prevent vibration from transferring directly into connected pipework. An air handling unit may require floor isolators or spring isolators, while suspended equipment can require acoustic or spring hangers. Cooling towers may require special attention to both operating vibration and structural support because of their rotating components, water mass, and rooftop location.

Industrial machinery presents additional challenges. Manufacturing equipment may generate periodic or broadband vibration that can affect adjacent machinery, production quality, or structural components. Precision applications can have particularly stringent vibration criteria because relatively small movements may affect measurement, imaging, fabrication, research, or process performance.

Healthcare facilities can present similarly demanding conditions. Hospitals, laboratories, medical centers, and research facilities may contain sensitive diagnostic or laboratory equipment close to mechanical rooms and building services. Vibration criteria may be established by the equipment manufacturer, owner, project specification, facility requirements, or a project-specific engineering assessment.

Data centers and mission-critical facilities also require coordinated mechanical-system design. Fans, pumps, cooling equipment, generators, and other rotating systems must operate reliably while limiting unwanted dynamic effects on sensitive spaces and equipment.

Marine applications introduce another set of conditions. Marine engines and shipboard machinery operate in environments where vibration, shock, corrosion, limited space, and multidirectional dynamic loads can all influence mounting selection.

In each case, an effective system considers more than the isolator itself. Equipment bases, inertia bases, support frames, floor construction, piping, ductwork, electrical connections, and adjacent structural elements must be considered as part of the vibration-control strategy.

Vibration Isolation, Seismic Restraint, Engineering Analysis, and Project Coordination

One of the most important distinctions in equipment support design is the difference between vibration isolation and seismic restraint.

Vibration isolation addresses normal operating conditions. Its purpose is to reduce the transmission of dynamic forces from equipment into the structure or from the structure into sensitive equipment.

Seismic restraint addresses earthquake-induced movement and load transfer. Depending on the application and governing project requirements, this can involve anchor bolts, structural attachments, seismic restraints, snubbers, restrained isolators, captive configurations, or MEP seismic bracing.

The same piece of equipment may require both. A chiller, pump, generator, or air handling unit may need vibration isolation during normal operation and seismic protection during an earthquake.

This creates a coordination challenge. A rigid seismic restraint installed without accounting for the intended movement of an isolation system can create a parallel load path and reduce the isolation system's effectiveness. Conversely, an isolation system that lacks appropriate seismic restraint may not satisfy applicable project requirements.

For projects subject to the International Building Code, California Building Code, ASCE 7, or applicable healthcare requirements, the relevant structural and nonstructural provisions should be evaluated based on the actual project scope and adopted code edition. These standards should not be represented as universal operating-vibration-limit standards. Operating vibration criteria may instead originate from equipment manufacturers, project specifications, owner requirements, facility criteria, environmental requirements, or engineering analysis.

Engineering Analysis and Verification

Engineering evaluation may include equipment operating data, excitation forces, mount reactions, natural frequencies, dynamic stiffness, transmissibility, structural response, vibration measurements, and frequency-spectrum or FFT analysis where appropriate.

Field verification can include checking actual mount loading, equipment alignment, clearances, flexible connections, restraint configuration, and measured vibration after installation.

BIM and Custom Fabrication

BIM 3D CAD modeling can help coordinate equipment dimensions, mounting points, isolator locations, structural supports, piping, ductwork, electrical connections, clearances, and seismic restraints before fabrication or installation.

When standard components do not accommodate equipment geometry or loading, custom-fabricated equipment bases, mounting plates, structural frames, isolation rails, brackets, and support assemblies may be developed. Material choices can include carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum, structural steel, or sheet metal, with galvanizing or powder coating selected when project conditions require additional corrosion protection.

Common Vibration Mounting Mistakes to Avoid

Many vibration problems originate not from a defective isolator but from an incomplete design or installation. One of the most common mistakes is selecting mounts solely from total equipment weight. Actual load distribution, center of gravity, mounting geometry, and operating conditions can substantially change the required capacity at individual support points.

Ignoring operating RPM is another frequent problem. An isolator cannot be evaluated properly without understanding the excitation frequencies it will encounter. Variable-speed equipment requires additional attention because its operating frequency can change during normal operation.

Insufficient static deflection can result in an isolation system with a natural frequency that does not provide the intended level of isolation. Conversely, selecting excessively flexible mounts without evaluating stability and movement can create unacceptable equipment motion.

Rigid connections are another concern. Piping, ductwork, conduit, structural attachments, and other components can create unintended mechanical bridges around an isolation system. Flexible connections must therefore be designed and installed as part of the overall system.

Environmental conditions should not be overlooked. An elastomer appropriate for an indoor mechanical room may not be appropriate for elevated temperatures, chemicals, outdoor exposure, or marine service.

Seismic coordination is equally important. Treating a vibration isolator as a seismic restraint, or adding rigid seismic hardware without evaluating the isolation movement, can create conflicting requirements.

Finally, field conditions matter. Actual equipment weight, mounting-point locations, structural conditions, clearances, and support reactions may differ from early design assumptions. Installation should therefore be checked against the engineered requirements rather than relying entirely on nominal drawings or catalog selections.

A robust vibration-control project establishes the requirements early, coordinates disciplines, verifies equipment data, and documents the final mounting arrangement clearly enough for contractors and inspectors to implement it correctly.

How to Approach a Vibration Mounting and Controls Project

A successful project begins by identifying the vibration source and defining what must be controlled. The engineering team can then establish equipment weight, operating speed, excitation frequencies, center of gravity, mounting geometry, support conditions, and the location of potentially sensitive spaces.

The next step is to identify the transmission path. This includes the equipment support, slab or structural framing, piping, ductwork, electrical connections, equipment bases, and other interfaces. Understanding these paths helps determine whether the primary problem is equipment vibration, structural response, structure-borne noise, or a combination of conditions.

Performance criteria should then be established from the appropriate sources. Depending on the project, these may include equipment manufacturer requirements, owner criteria, project specifications, facility requirements, industry-specific criteria, or engineering analysis.

With those inputs established, engineers can evaluate spring, rubber, wire rope, acoustic, floor, or other isolation technologies. Selection should address static and dynamic loading, natural frequency, transmissibility, damping, environmental conditions, stability, displacement, and maintenance requirements.

The system must then be coordinated with structural and MEP design. Flexible connectors should accommodate the expected movement without creating bypass paths. Seismic restraint should be incorporated where required, with the interaction between seismic hardware and vibration isolation understood.

For complex equipment, BIM 3D CAD modeling can provide a practical method for coordinating mounting geometry, support steel, equipment clearances, piping, ductwork, and restraint locations. Custom fabrication can then be based on defined engineering loads, connection details, tolerances, and equipment dimensions.

The overall workflow is:

Vibration Source → Operating Conditions → Excitation Frequencies → Isolation Analysis → Mount Selection → Structural/MEP Coordination → Seismic Coordination → BIM/CAD → Fabrication → Installation → Inspection → Performance Verification

This process is particularly valuable when the project involves hospitals, laboratories, data centers, industrial machinery, marine equipment, large HVAC systems, or retrofit conditions where a standard catalog arrangement may not address the complete engineering problem.

Conclusion: Engineering Vibration Mounting for Reliable Equipment Performance

Effective vibration mounting and controls begin with understanding the dynamic behavior of the equipment and the structure to which it is connected. A vibration isolator is not simply a flexible component placed beneath a machine. Its performance depends on equipment mass, load distribution, excitation frequency, natural frequency, static deflection, dynamic stiffness, damping, mounting geometry, structural response, and the surrounding transmission paths.

The most appropriate solution can vary significantly between applications. Spring isolators may be appropriate for equipment requiring substantial deflection and low natural frequency. Rubber and elastomeric mounts can provide compact isolation for a broad range of mechanical equipment. Wire rope systems can address multidirectional vibration and demanding environments, while acoustic hangers and floor isolation systems can control transmission through suspended and structural building elements.

Application conditions are equally important. HVAC equipment, rotating machinery, precision equipment, healthcare facilities, industrial plants, marine engines, and mission-critical infrastructure each introduce different combinations of loading, frequency, environmental exposure, stability, and performance requirements.

A complete design must also account for interfaces beyond the mount itself. Rigid piping, ductwork, electrical connections, structural attachments, and improperly coordinated seismic restraints can compromise an otherwise well-designed isolation system. Vibration isolation and seismic restraint should therefore be treated as separate but coordinated engineering requirements.

For U.S. construction projects, applicable provisions of IBC, CBC, ASCE 7, HCAI requirements, project specifications, manufacturer requirements, and owner criteria should be evaluated according to the project's jurisdiction and scope. No single standard should be assumed to establish every operating-vibration criterion.

The strongest vibration-control solutions connect analysis, product selection, structural coordination, BIM/CAD modeling, fabrication, installation, and verification. That integrated approach is particularly important when equipment is heavy, sensitive, dynamically active, difficult to access, or installed within a facility where vibration performance directly affects operations.

Frequently Asked Questions About Vibration Mounting and Controls

What is vibration mounting?

Vibration mounting is the engineered use of mounts, isolators, bases, hangers, and support assemblies to control the transfer of dynamic forces between equipment and a supporting structure. The objective is generally to reduce transmitted vibration while maintaining adequate equipment stability and support.

The appropriate mounting approach depends on equipment weight, operating speed, excitation frequency, center of gravity, mounting geometry, structural conditions, and required performance. Common technologies include spring vibration isolators, rubber and elastomeric mounts, wire rope isolators, acoustic hangers, and floor isolation systems.

How do vibration mounts reduce transmitted vibration?

Vibration mounts introduce a controlled mechanical interface between the equipment and its support. By changing the stiffness and dynamic characteristics of that interface, the system can reduce the amount of dynamic force transmitted into the structure.

For many isolation systems, the relationship between equipment excitation frequency and isolator natural frequency is fundamental. Effective isolation generally requires sufficient frequency separation, while damping helps control resonance and excessive movement.

The complete system must also be considered because rigid piping, ductwork, conduit, or structural attachments can create alternate transmission paths.

What type of vibration isolator is best for HVAC equipment?

There is no single isolator that is universally appropriate for every HVAC application. Selection depends on the equipment type, operating speed, weight, support arrangement, static deflection requirements, movement limits, installation location, and project criteria.

Fans, pumps, chillers, cooling towers, and air handling units may use different combinations of spring isolators, rubber mounts, inertia bases, isolation rails, floor isolators, spring hangers, or acoustic hangers.

The equipment manufacturer's operating data and mounting requirements should be evaluated along with structural and MEP conditions before selecting the system.

What is the difference between spring and rubber vibration isolators?

Spring isolators generally provide relatively low stiffness and can achieve substantial static deflection, making them useful where low natural frequencies and significant vibration isolation are required. Rubber and elastomeric mounts are typically more compact and can provide stiffness and damping characteristics suitable for many equipment applications.

The comparison should not be reduced to which material is inherently better. Engineers should consider frequency requirements, equipment loading, movement, environmental conditions, durability, geometry, and stability.

A rubber mount designed for one application may have completely different performance characteristics from another elastomeric mount, just as spring systems vary according to spring rate and configuration.

When should wire rope isolators be used?

Wire rope isolators can be useful when multidirectional isolation, environmental durability, compact construction, or resistance to demanding operating conditions is important. They are used in various industrial, marine, aerospace, electronics, and equipment applications.

Their suitability depends on the required load capacity, displacement, frequency range, environmental conditions, mounting geometry, and performance criteria. Wire rope should therefore be selected from the actual dynamic requirements rather than simply because the application involves heavy equipment.

How does static deflection affect vibration isolation?

Static deflection is the amount an isolator moves under the supported static load. In spring-based isolation systems, static deflection is closely related to spring stiffness and natural frequency.

Increasing static deflection can allow a lower natural frequency, which may improve isolation when operating frequencies are sufficiently separated from the isolation-system natural frequency. However, excessive flexibility can create stability or movement problems.

Engineers must balance isolation performance with equipment stability, load capacity, displacement limits, clearances, and operating conditions.

Can vibration isolation and seismic restraint be used together?

Yes. Equipment can require vibration isolation during normal operation and seismic restraint for earthquake conditions. A chiller, pump, generator, air handling unit, or other mechanical system may therefore incorporate both types of protection.

The critical issue is coordination. Seismic restraints must accommodate the intended behavior of the isolation system and should not unintentionally create rigid bypass paths that undermine normal vibration isolation.

Applicable seismic requirements should be evaluated based on the project's adopted code, jurisdiction, equipment classification, structural design, and project specifications.

Do vibration mounts require engineering calculations?

Not every simple mounting application requires the same level of engineering analysis, but complex equipment and critical facilities can require detailed evaluation. Calculations may address support reactions, spring rates, natural frequency, static deflection, dynamic stiffness, excitation frequency, transmissibility, structural response, and equipment stability.

Additional analysis may be appropriate for high-speed machinery, sensitive equipment, large HVAC systems, irregular mounting configurations, retrofit projects, and installations where structural vibration is a significant concern.

The required level of analysis should be established from project criteria, equipment requirements, engineering scope, and applicable specifications.

How do you select vibration mounts for rotating equipment?

Start with accurate equipment information, including operating weight, rotational speed, excitation characteristics, center of gravity, mounting-point locations, and support geometry.

Engineers should evaluate the relationship between operating frequency and the isolation system's natural frequency, then determine the required load capacity and dynamic characteristics at each mounting point. Startup, shutdown, variable-speed operation, harmonics, and potential unbalanced forces may also need consideration.

The supporting structure and connected piping or ductwork should be evaluated because they can influence the final dynamic response.

Can existing mechanical equipment be retrofitted with vibration isolation?

Yes, retrofit vibration isolation can be practical when the existing equipment, structure, and available installation space are properly evaluated. Retrofit work can be more challenging than new construction because existing slabs, framing, equipment bases, piping, ductwork, and access conditions are already established.

The evaluation should consider existing equipment weight, actual mounting conditions, operating vibration, structural response, available clearance, utility connections, and installation sequencing.

Equipment replacement can also create a new vibration-control requirement if the replacement machine has different operating speed, weight, dynamic forces, or mounting geometry.

Are vibration control requirements covered by ASCE 7 or the building code?

IBC, CBC, and ASCE 7 can be relevant to structural, seismic, equipment-support, and nonstructural design requirements, but they should not be treated as universal standards for every operating-vibration limit.

Acceptable operating vibration levels may instead be established by equipment manufacturers, project specifications, owners, facility criteria, environmental requirements, industry-specific requirements, or engineering analysis.

For California healthcare projects under applicable HCAI jurisdiction, additional healthcare-related structural and project requirements may apply. The Engineer of Record and project team should determine which requirements govern the specific installation.

When are custom vibration mounting systems necessary?

Custom mounting systems can become necessary when standard components cannot accommodate equipment geometry, support reactions, load distribution, clearances, environmental conditions, or project-specific structural requirements.

Examples include custom equipment bases, structural support frames, mounting plates, isolation rails, brackets, and fabricated assemblies. Custom fabrication can be especially useful for large industrial equipment, irregular equipment footprints, retrofit conditions, marine applications, and coordinated MEP installations.

The fabrication should follow defined engineering loads, connection requirements, material specifications, tolerances, and equipment dimensions. Custom fabrication is most effective when integrated with engineering and BIM/CAD coordination rather than treated as a substitute for system design.
